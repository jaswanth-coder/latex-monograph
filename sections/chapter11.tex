\chapter{Game Theory Applications}
\label{sec:chapter11}

In modern wireless networks, autonomous network entities---such as mobile user equipments (UEs), wireless local area network (WLAN) stations, and small cell base stations---frequently compete for shared, scarce radio resources. Centralized coordination of massive numbers of distributed nodes incurs severe signaling overhead, latency penalties, and scalability bottlenecks. Consequently, decentralized and distributed decision-making frameworks are essential.

Game theory provides a rigorous mathematical framework for modeling and analyzing strategic interactions among independent, rational decision-makers. In these formulations, each node acts as a player seeking to maximize its individual performance objective (utility function), taking into account the anticipated actions of competing nodes. A classical problem in networking is to design efficient techniques for fair allocation of scarce resources (such as spectrum, power, and computing capacity) among contenders. In particular, the use of game theory in addressing resource allocation issues in wireless networks (e.g., congestion control, power control, medium access control, routing, and mobile edge computing) has grown significantly.

Recently, non-conventional game-theoretic models (such as Minority Games) have attracted significant attention to overcome several practical limitations of conventional game models in large-scale networks, including:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(a)] excessive signaling overhead related to data exchange between players,
    \item[(b)] high energy consumption due to computational complexity,
    \item[(c)] idealized assumptions of complete rationality among distributed players, and
    \item[(d)] theoretical complexity in characterizing large equilibrium sets.
\end{enumerate}

This chapter explores game-theoretic modeling in wireless communications, focusing on non-cooperative games, the minority game framework for mobile data offloading, and distributed contention control.

\section{Game-Theoretic Modeling in Wireless Networks}
\label{sec:gt_modeling}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Strategic Form Games and Nash Equilibrium}
A strategic (normal) form game is formally defined by the triplet $\mathcal{G} = (\mathcal{N}, \{\mathcal{S}_i\}_{i \in \mathcal{N}}, \{u_i\}_{i \in \mathcal{N}})$, where:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathcal{N} = \{1, 2, \dots, N\}$ is the finite set of participating players (e.g., contending stations or mobile devices).
    \item $\mathcal{S}_i$ denotes the strategy set (action space) available to player $i$. An action profile is represented as $\mathbf{s} = (s_i, \mathbf{s}_{-i}) \in \mathcal{S} = \prod_{i=1}^N \mathcal{S}_i$, where $\mathbf{s}_{-i}$ represents the strategies chosen by all players other than $i$.
    \item $u_i(\mathbf{s}) : \mathcal{S} \to \mathbb{R}$ is the utility (payoff) function of player $i$, reflecting its throughput, energy consumption, delay penalty, or monetary cost.
\end{itemize}

A central solution concept in non-cooperative games is the \emph{Nash Equilibrium} (NE). A strategy profile $\mathbf{s}^* = (s_1^*, s_2^*, \dots, s_N^*)$ constitutes a pure-strategy Nash equilibrium if no player can unilaterally improve its utility by deviating from its chosen strategy:
\begin{equation}
    u_i(s_i^*, \mathbf{s}_{-i}^*) \ge u_i(s_i, \mathbf{s}_{-i}^*), \quad \forall s_i \in \mathcal{S}_i, \quad \forall i \in \mathcal{N}.
\end{equation}
When pure equilibria do not exist, players may adopt mixed strategies, selecting actions according to probability distributions $p_i(s_i)$, leading to mixed-strategy Nash equilibria.

\subsection{Pricing Mechanisms and Incentive Design}
In competitive wireless environments, selfish behavior often leads to the ``tragedy of the commons,'' where aggressive spectrum access collapses overall network efficiency (e.g., severe collisions in random access channels). To guide selfish nodes toward socially desirable equilibria, network operators introduce pricing mechanisms. The net utility function of player $i$ becomes:
\begin{equation}
    U_i(\mathbf{s}) = u_i(\mathbf{s}) - P_i(\mathbf{s}),
\end{equation}
where $P_i(\mathbf{s})$ is the cost charged by the network. By dynamically adjusting price parameters based on network congestion or interference levels, distributed nodes can be steered toward Pareto-optimal resource allocation.

\section{Mobile Data Offloading via Minority Game Framework}
\label{sec:minority_game_offloading}
% ============================================================

\subsection{The Data Offloading Problem}
The explosion of mobile data traffic driven by high-definition video streaming, augmented reality, and cloud computing places severe strain on cellular licensed spectrum. Mobile data offloading channelizes a fraction of cellular traffic over complementary, unlicensed wireless local area networks (e.g., IEEE 802.11 Wi-Fi).

However, uncoordinated offloading can lead to herd behavior: when numerous mobile devices simultaneously offload their traffic to a nearby Wi-Fi Access Point (AP), severe contention and collisions degrade Wi-Fi throughput below that of the cellular network. Conversely, if too few devices offload, cellular spectrum remains heavily congested while Wi-Fi resources are underutilized.

\subsection{The Minority Game (MG) Formulation}

Minority Game (MG) is a type of non-cooperative games, which has recently gained 
the research community's attention to model distributed resource allocation based problems. 
The original version of MG was mathematically conceptualized by Challet and Zhang \cite{Challet23}, 
being inspired by the famous El Farol bar problem \cite{Arthur22} by Arthur. 
In the binary version of MG, an odd number of agents select between two alternatives in 
each round of the game. The group of agents, which is minority in strength, is declared as 
the winner of that round. Therefore, the aim of each agent is to fall into the minority group, in each round.
The enriched literature of MG includes several variants of MG like, Evolutionary Minority Game, Minority Game with exponential learning, Multiple-choice Minority Game, and so on. There has been a growing interest in the statistical mechanics-based analysis of MG to study its dynamics. Over the last decade, extensive research related to MG has been carried out by researchers from the econophysics community \cite{MGBook_33}. 

To motivate the reader on how the framework of MG can be used to solve distributed resource allocation problem, we take the example of mobile data offloading. Consider a set of cellular users in the vicinity of a WiFi hotspot, attempting to offload some of its traffic through WLAN, since it is not only free of cost but it offers high bandwidth. If a minority of the users attempt to offload, they get a good share of offered bandwidth and hence considered as winners. On the other hand, if at any given time, a majority of users attempt to offload, they not only get poor QoS but also lose energy due to contention. Here again the minority group that differ to offload win by at least  conserving the energy. Thus, the goal of every user in a group that contend for scarce resource is to be a part of the minority group. Therefore, the resource allocation problem can be suitably formulated as a Minority game.

To achieve decentralized, self-organizing load balancing without continuous central signaling, the offloading process can be modeled using the \emph{Minority Game} (MG) paradigm. Originally introduced in econophysics, the minority game models systems where $N$ players repeatedly choose between two alternative actions, and those who choose the less crowded (minority) option win a payoff.

In the context of mobile data offloading, let $N$ mobile users choose between two transmission technologies:
\begin{equation}
    a_i \in \{-1, +1\},
\end{equation}
where $a_i = -1$ corresponds to retaining the transmission on the cellular link, and $a_i = +1$ corresponds to offloading to Wi-Fi. Let $A(t) = \sum_{i=1}^N a_i(t)$ denote the aggregate action sum at decision round $t$. The minority outcome occurs when the chosen alternative has fewer occupants than a defined capacity threshold.

\subsection{Exponential Learning Dynamics and Target Pricing}
To adapt their decisions under non-stationary traffic, nodes implement an exponential learning reinforcement scheme. Each node maintains virtual scores $U_{i, k}(t)$ for a portfolio of heuristic decision strategies $k \in \mathcal{K}_i$. At each round, the strategy is selected according to a Gibbs-Boltzmann soft-max probability distribution:
\begin{equation}
    \pi_{i, k}(t) = \frac{\exp\left( \frac{U_{i, k}(t)}{T_c} \right)}{\sum_{k' \in \mathcal{K}_i} \exp\left( \frac{U_{i, k'}(t)}{T_c} \right)},
\end{equation}
where $T_c$ is a temperature coefficient governing the exploration-versus-exploitation trade-off.

After transmission, strategies that favored the winning (uncongested) resource receive score increments, while those that led to congestion are penalized. To align user decisions with operator revenue and QoS targets, a \emph{Target Pricing scheme} is incorporated:
\begin{equation}
    b(t+1) = b(t) + \beta \left( \bar{S}_{\text{WiFi}}(t) - S^*_{\text{target}} \right),
\end{equation}
where $b(t)$ is the dynamic unit price parameter, $\beta > 0$ is a learning step size, and $S^*_{\text{target}}$ is the optimal target Wi-Fi throughput.

By coupling minority game dynamics with dynamic pricing, distributed mobile nodes self-balance across cellular and Wi-Fi networks in heterogeneous environments, stabilizing system throughput near theoretical capacity even under dynamic user arrival and departure patterns.

\section{Non-Cooperative Contention and Power Control}
\label{sec:noncoop_contention_power}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Contention Window Adaptation Games}
In random access protocols such as CSMA/CA, nodes choose their backoff parameters (minimum contention window $CW_{\min}$). If modeled as a non-cooperative game where each node seeks to maximize individual throughput:
\begin{equation}
    u_i(\tau_i, \boldsymbol{\tau}_{-i}) = \frac{\tau_i \prod_{j \neq i} (1 - \tau_j)}{\bar{L}_{\text{slot}}},
\end{equation}
nodes have an incentive to transmit with transmission probability $\tau_i \to 1$ ($CW_{\min} \to 0$). This dominant strategy results in continuous collisions, driving aggregate network utility to zero. Game-theoretic penalty functions and utility proportional fair pricing enforce socially efficient backoff parameters, preventing malicious starvation.

\subsection{Power Control and Interference Games}
In co-channel wireless deployments, transmitters choose power levels $p_i \in [0, P_{\max}]$ to maximize their Signal-to-Interference-plus-Noise Ratio (SINR):
\begin{equation}
    \gamma_i(\mathbf{p}) = \frac{h_{ii} p_i}{\sigma^2 + \sum_{j \neq i} h_{ji} p_j}.
\end{equation}
A standard utility function balances achievable rate and power dissipation:
\begin{equation}
    u_i(\mathbf{p}) = \ln(1 + \gamma_i(\mathbf{p})) - \alpha_i p_i,
\end{equation}
where $\alpha_i$ represents the unit energy cost. The best-response update rule yields supermodular games whose fixed points guarantee the existence and uniqueness of the Nash equilibrium power allocation vector.
