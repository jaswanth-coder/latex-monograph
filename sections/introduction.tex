\section{Stochastic Processes}
A stochastic process is a mathematical structure defined as indexed family of random variables.
Stochastic processes are widely used as mathematical models of systems and phenomena that
appear to vary in a random manner.The index of a SP is also referred as its parameter. In most cases it may denote time

Inherently we assume that the random variables have the same state space and are real val-
ued functions defined over the same $\sigma$-algebra constructed from a event space. Physically it
means the RVs correspond to the same random experiment ( physical phenomenon ) but con-
ducted ( observed ) at say different time or space points ( that constitute the index set ). f we repeat the Bernoulli trial again, its outcome can be used to define another random
variable, say X2. Arranging all of them together X1, X2, X3,... and giving index, {1, 2, 3, ...}
we get a stochastic process

We know that a random variable can have discrete or continuous state space. To be specific a discrete valued RV takes values from a finite or countable infinite set. We also saw that the index or parameter of a SP can be discrete or continuous. It follows that we can categorize the SPs into four categories based on the nature of the state space of its RVs and the nature of its parameter. The following table illustrates the classification of the SPs.
 
\begin{center}
 \begin{tabular}{||p{3cm}|p{5cm}|p{5cm}||} 
 \hline
 \textbf{Index / state space} & \textbf{Discrete} & \textbf{Continuous} \\ [0.5ex] 
 \hline\hline
 \textbf{Discrete} & Discrete parameter, Discrete state stochastic process & Discrete parameter,  continuous state stochastic process  \\ 
 \hline
 \textbf{Continuous} & Discrete state, Continuous parameter stochastic process & Continuous state, Continuous parameter stochastic process  \\
 \hline

 
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
An example of  Discrete parameter, Discrete state stochastic process is a sequence of Bernoulli trials such as sequence of coin tossing denoted as
{Xn : n = 1, 2, 3}, with X = 1 for success and X =0 for a failure.
An example of  continuous  parameter, Discrete state stochastic process   is  the number of customers who have entered into a market till time t
$\{A(t) \mid t \ge 0\}$
At any given time t, the random variable A(t), denote the number of customer arrived till time
t. A(t) is an discrete quantity. It can take only non negative and integer values.

An example of discrete parameter continious state stochastic processes is the time inter-
val between the arrival of successive customers, {t1, t2, t3, .....} or {tn|n = 1, 2, 3, ..}, where tn
is the time interval between nth customer and previous customer. Here index is discrete and the
state space of the RV, time is continuous.

Continuous state and continuous time stochastic process is the Brownian motion, the motion of a pollen grain suspended in water. 

\section{Discrete Time Markov Chains : Basics and properties}
In this section we will discuss the Discrete Time Markov Chains abbreviated henceforth as DTMC. To reiterate the parameter of the DTMC is time and it is discrete in nature taking finite or countably infinite values. The state space of DTMC is also discrete, hence the adjective "chain". Further it obeys the Markov property. To understand the Markov property in the context of DTMC, let us take an example of DTMC.
Consider a sequence of Bernoulli trials of a fair coin. The probability of getting a Head (H) is 0.5 and that of getting a Tail(T) is 0.5. For example a realization of the  sequence of trials may look like \{H,H,T,T,H,H,T...\}.To define a discrete RV, let us associate the real numbers "1" or "0"  with the occurrence of the events Head and Tail respectively. 

Let us  denote the  Random variables corresponding to successive trials as as ${X_1, X_2, X_3 ...}$.
Let $S_n$ define the cumulative profit until and including the $n^{th}$ trial.  For any n,  $S_n$ is a random variable and it is given by 
\begin{equation}
    S_n = X_1 + X_2 + ... +X_n
\end{equation}.
\begin{center}
$\{S_n|n=1,2,3...\}$ 
\end{center}
is a DTMC as follows. If we are in a given state, at any given time, for predicting, we are concerned with the values at the instant and not the values which were previously there.
\begin{equation*}
    P(S_8=4|S_3=3,S_2=2,...)=P(S_8=4|S_3=3)
\end{equation*}

Let us consider a Discrete Time Markov chain with infinite number of states $\left\{X_n;\;n=1,2,3,\dots\right\}$ such that

\begin{equation*}
    P(X_{n+1}=j|X_n=i,X_{n-1}=k,X_{n-2}=l,\dots)=P(X_{n+1}=j|X_n=i)=p_{ij}
\end{equation*}
Given the history of states traversed till time $n$, i.e., $X_n,\;X_{n-1},\;X_{n-2}\;\dots$, the next state $X_{n+1}$ depends only on the most recent state $X_n$.\textbf{Time Homogeneous Process:} If the state transition $(X_{n+1}=j|X_n=i)$ is independent of $n$ and depends only on the time difference, then the Markov chain is said to be a time homogeneous markov chain.
\subsection{Transition Probability Matrix (TPM): } $\mathbb{P}=[p_{ij}]_{s\times s}, \; 1\leq i,j \leq s$, is the one step transition probability matrix when there are $s$ number of states in the Markov chain. TPM can be defined for a discrete time Markov chain irrespective of the number of states in the Markov chain being finite or infinite.
  
  \begin{equation*}
      \mathbb{P}=\begin{bmatrix}
      p_{11}&p_{12}&p_{13}&\dots & p_{1s}\\
      p_{21}&p_{22}&p_{23}&\dots & p_{2s}\\
      \vdots&\vdots&\vdots&\dots&\vdots\\
      p_{s1}&p_{s2}&p_{s3}&\dots&p_{ss}
      \end{bmatrix}
  \end{equation*}

  \subsection{State Occupancy Probability}
  State occupancy probability is defined as the probability that a particular state occupied at $n^{th}$ time instance. Probability that a state $j$  is occupies at $n^{th}$ time instance is denoted as $\pi_{j}^{n}$ as is defined as follows.
  \begin{equation*}
      \pi_j^n=P(X_n=j)
  \end{equation*}
  
  \textbf{Note:} Occupancy probability is different from the transition probability. 
  
  State occupancy vector is the collection of occupancy probabilities of all states in the markov chain and is given as follows 
  \begin{equation*}
      \overline{\pi}^n=[\pi^n_1\; \pi^n_2\; \pi^n_3\; \dots\; \pi^n_s]
  \end{equation*}
  
  State occupancy vector follows all properties of a probability mass function (PMF) as listed below.
  
  \begin{enumerate}
      \item $\pi^n_j\geq 0$ $\forall j$
      \item $\sum_j \pi^n_j =1$
  \end{enumerate}
  
  First condition tells that the probability of any state being occupied at a given instance is non-negative. Second condition shows the stochastic property i.e., at any given time any one of the state should be occupied.  State occupancy probability at $n+1^{th}$ instance can be calculated from state occupancy vector at $n^{th}$ instance and the transition probability matrix $\mathbb{P}$ as follows.
  \begin{align*}
       \pi_j^{n+1}=P(X_{n+1}=j)&=\sum_iP(X_n=i)P(X_{n+1}=j|X_n=i)\\
       &=\sum_i \pi^n_i p_{ij} \qquad\qquad \forall j
  \end{align*}
  
  From the above equation, the state occupancy vector at $n+1^{th}$ instance is determined as follows,
  
  \begin{equation*}
      \overline{\pi}^{n+1}=\overline{\pi}^n \mathbb{P}
  \end{equation*}

\section{Markov Decision Processes }


In this section we study about the Markov Decision Processes, abbreviated as MDP. MDP provides a mathematical framework for modeling decision making in situations where outcomes are partly random and partly under the control of a decision maker.
Some Applications : 
\begin {enumerate}
\item when to replace/inspect machines/components based on age, condition.
\item how much to produce based on demand.
\item self-driving cars
\item When and how much stocks to be purchased.
\end{enumerate}
 Let us have a discrete time stochastic processes with finite steps given as $\{X_n|n=1,2,3...\}$, where $\{X_n\}$ for $n=1,2,3...$ are random variables defined at time 1,2,3....

Assuming $\{X_n|n=1,2,3...\}$ to be a DTMC, we know  the following equation, 
\begin{equation*}
    P(X_{n+1}=x_{n+1}|X_{n}=x_{n},X_{n-1}=x_{n-1},X_{n-2}=x_{n-2},X_{n-3}=x_{n-3}...)=P(X_{n+1}=x_{n+1}|X_{n}=x_{n})
\end{equation*}
 
State is linked to an external process, and this external process performs an action($a$).
Action Space($A$) is the set of all actions performed by the external agency on the system, $a \in A$.
In other words in every step we are supposed to take an action. The action performed depends upon the state of the DTMC at that time. There are only a finite number of actions that are allowed to be taken and they form the action space $A$.

 When an action is performed the DTMC  moves from state $x_n$ to $x_{n+1}$ depending on the action. This is can be written in the equation form as follows, 
\begin{equation*}
    P(X_{n+1}=j|X_{n}=i, A_n=a_n)=p_{ij}(a)
\end{equation*}
The transition from state $i$ to $j$ happens depending upon the current state($i$) as well as the action($a$). In MDPs, given the current state($i$) alone, the transition for the next state cannot happen, without the knowledge of the action taken. In every state, we need to perform an action. The sequence of actions performed are RVs $\{A_1,A_2,A_3...\}$, whose realizations are $\{a_1, a_2,... \}$. In a normal DTMC the transition probability $P_{ij}$ depends only of the former and the later state $i$ and $j$. In MDP $P_{ij}$ are functions of the action $a$ taken. Thus the TPM itself is a function of time and we have one TPM for each action.

Example 1: Let us consider we have three urns with some blue, red and yellow coloured balls. We have to randomly select any one ball from any one of the urns, note its colour and put it back. 

The total number of red balls that has been accumulated (let it be $n_r$)is the state. Action is deciding which urn to choose. $n_r$ depends upon the action, i.e. the urn we choose.

Example 2: Let us assume, that you are a Marketing Manager. You have three states of sales condition,
\begin{center}

The Sales condition, Good, Medium and Poor are the states. As a Marketing Manager your action is to do something such that the sale improves and more than that the profit improves. Suppose some product costs \$c, you may give 10\% discount on the product and observe.  This offer may decide the sales condition. Actions might include "Giving an Discount", "spend on advertisement",changing the price of the commodity, "increase/decrease the inventory forecasting the sales" etc. Suppose giving an offer increases the sale might mover the state to a "good" position. The next action which you may take is that you to increase the cost of the product from \$c to \$c$+\delta$. What you observe is that as the cost increases the sale reduces, but the net profit may increase or decrease. 

Let us now define the term \textbf{Policy},it is a way of specifying what action should be taken when in a given state. It is represented as $\beta_i(a)$, where action $a$ is taken when in state $i$. 

The first policy we can think of is  a deterministic policy. If the action taken $a$ is fixed for all states then the MDP is just a DTMC with transition probabilities as $P_{ij}(a)$. 

Next we consider the case wherein in every state the action taken fixed whenever the state is visited, but the action taken can vary from state to state. Let $a_{i}$  be the action taken in state $i$. Transition Probability Matrix(TPM), of the MDP will be such that its ith row will  be the $i$th row of the TPM corresponding to the action $a_{i}$.
Deterministic Action Policy always gives an ordinary DTMC. 

Now we consider  Random Policy in which different actions can be chosen in a given state  and they are specified using a probability distribution. That is the probability of taking action $a$ in state $i$ is,
\begin{equation*}
    P(A_n=a|X_n=i)=\beta_i(a)
\end{equation*}

For the policy to be a valid policy the following two conditions must be satisfied.
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\beta_i(a) \geq 0$, as they are probabilities
    \item $\sum_{a\in A}\beta_i(a)=1$, in a given state $i$ under finite set of actions, one or the other action has to be taken up. 
\end{itemize}

$P_{ij}(a) \rightarrow$ probability that you go from state $i$ to state $j$ under a given action $a$.

Over a long period, the states visited or the actions taken have settled down. THe probability of going from state $i$ to state $j$ is then,
\begin{equation*}
    P(X_n+1=j|X_n=i)=\sum_a \beta_i(a)P_{ij}(a)
    \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*}
    \Pi_{i,a}=P(X_n=i, A_n=a)
\end{equation*}
The following conditions must hold, $\Pi_{i,a}$
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\Pi_{i,a} \geq 0$, probability of being in state $i$ and performing action $a$.
    \item $\sum_i \sum_a \Pi_{i,a}=1$, $\Pi_{i,a}$ is not a vector, it is a matrix.
    
\textbf{Question: For a valid probability distribution, is this condition sufficient?}

$\sum_i \sum_a \Pi_{i,a}$ means that the state $i$ is doing an action $a$ and then moves to state $j$.
\item $P(X_n=j)=\Pi_j=\sum_a\Pi_{j,a}=\sum_i \sum_a \Pi_{i,a}P_{i,j}(a)$
\end{itemize}

Suppose for a given Markov Decision Process, some policy is taken. Suppose we find its limiting probabilities $\Pi_{i,a}$, which obeys the above stated three conditions.  Then, what is the policy which leads once we know the $\Pi_{i,a}$ ?

\begin{align*}
    \beta_i(a)&=P(A_n=a|X_n=i)\\
    &=\frac{P(A_n=a,X_n=i)}{P(X_n=i)}\\
    &=\frac{\Pi_{i,a}}{\sum_a\Pi_{i,a}}
\end{align*}
$\therefore$, If we have, a vector with n states and n actions, $[\Pi_{0,1} \Pi_{0,2} \dots \Pi_{0,M} \Pi_{1,M} \dots \Pi_{n,n}]$
For a given MDP, many such vectors can be found. We are not simply deciding on the policy, but the target is to also achieve optimization and for that a reward is associated with every action. The reward associated in performing a particular action $a$ from a given state $i$ is given as $R(i,a)$.

\begin{align*}
    \text{Total Reward in n time steps}=\sum_{i=1}^nR(i,a)
\end{align*}
Suppose the policy $\beta$ is used then,
\begin{align*}
    \text{Average Reward per unit time}=\overline{R}=\lim_{x \to +\infty}\frac{\sum_{i=1}^nR_\beta(x_i,a_i)}{n}
\end{align*}
The reward $R_\beta(x_i,a_i)$ is a function of the policy used. Another way of getting the average reward per unit time is to find the expectation of the reward function, i.e., $\mathbb{E}[R_\beta]$ for a given policy $\beta$.

\begin{align*}
    \mathbb{E}[R_\beta]=\sum_i \sum_a \Pi_{i,a}R_\beta(i,a)
\end{align*}
For a given discrete time stochastic process, you chose the policy which maximizes the reward. 
\begin{align*}
    \textbf{Optimization Strategy}=maximize(\sum_i \sum_a \Pi_{i,a}R_\beta(i,a))
\end{align*}
By choosing the $\Pi_{i,a}$ values from the policies, subjected to the previously mentioned three conditions. We have to maximize, $E[R_{\beta}]$. It is a linear programming problem.

From the definition of Markov Property, we know, actions depend on the current state. Let us consider a maintenance problem, where "0" indicates the machine is in very good state, "1" indicates that the machine has slight problem, "2" indicates that the machine has some serious problem but it can still work and finally, "3" indicates that the machine cannot work and the only solution is to replace it. The three possible actions are to do nothing, perform overhaul, and replace. The profit is experienced from manufacturing and selling. Loss is experienced when under going overhauling and replacing, as there is no manufacturing.     

Let us model the problem. When in state 0, machine is in good state, action is to "do nothing" and experience no loss. In state 1, manufacturing can go on, loss is 1000, as minor defects hamper the production. In state 2 overhauling is needed, and loss is 4000. In state 3, the machine cannot work and no production causes loss of 2000 and overhauling cost is 4000, so the total loss while in state 3 is 6000. In states 1, 2 and 3 you can manufacture with different capacities and get a profit of 
3000, 2000, and 1000 respectively.
%We can have a Markov Chain for the state transition diagram with two arcs based on the different actions taken. For this problem the policy is a deterministic policy. 

Towards an LP formulation: 

\begin{align*}
    \textbf{Optimization Strategy}=maximize(\sum_i \sum_a \Pi_{i,a}R_\beta(i,a))
\end{align*}
subjected to 

\begin{itemize}
    \item $\beta_i(a) \geq 0$, 
    \item $\sum_{a\in A}\beta_i(a)=1$, 
    
    \item $\Pi_j=\sum_a\Pi_{j,a}$
\end{itemize}

MDP can be solved using dynamic programming.

\section{Exponential Distribution}
In this section, we will discuss about Continuous Time Markov Chain. First we will discuss the  i) Exponential distribution and ii) Poisson Stochastic Process are crucial for developing the formalism of CTMC.

\textbf{Exponential Distribution:} A continuous valued random variable is said to be exponentially distributed with parameters $\lambda$ ( $\lambda \geq 0$) if its probability density function (PDF ) takes the form 

\begin{align*}
    f_X(x)=\begin{cases} 
    \lambda e^{-\lambda x}, & x \geq 0\\
0, & x < 0
\end{cases}
\end{align*}
Recall the defenition of the PDF;  $\{P(x \leq X \leq x+dx)\}=f_X(x)dx$.
Cumulative Distribution Function(CDF) is defined as $F_X(x)=P(X \leq x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f(x)dx$.
Let us find the CDF for a random variable X, which is exponentially distributed with parameter $\lambda$.
\begin{align*}
    F_X(x)=\begin{cases} 
    1- e^{-\lambda x}, & x \geq 0\\
0, & x < 0
\end{cases}
\end{align*}

Complementary Cumulative Distribution Function(CCDF) is defined as  $1-F_X(x)$. CCDF for an exponentially distributed random variable is given as,
\begin{align*}
    1-F_X(x)=\begin{cases} 
    e^{-\lambda x}, & x \geq 0\\
0, & x < 0
\end{cases}
\end{align*}
The expected values of the exponentially distributed RV with parameter $\lambda$ can be easily derived to be 1 / $\lambda$.

\textbf{Memoryless Property in Exponentially Distributed Random Variables}

Suppose that the amount of time one spends at a bus stop is exponentially distributed with mean five minutes, that is $\frac{1}{\lambda}$. What is the probability that a person will spend more than 3 minutes at the bus stop, given that he is already waited at the bus stop for 4 minutes? 

We want to know the nature of the remaining time. 
\begin{align*}
    P(X>s+t|X>s)&=\frac{P(X>s+t,X>s)}{P(X>s)}\\
    &=\frac{P(X>s+t)}{P(X>s)}\\
    &=\frac{e^{-\lambda(t+s)}}{e^{-\lambda t}}\\
    &=e^{-\lambda s}\\
    &=P(X>t)
\end{align*}

In other words, the distribution of the remaining time  is same as the original distribution, that is the process does not remember that it has been waiting for time t.This property is called the memoryless property.

\subsection*{Poisson Stochastic Process}
A discrete state continuous time counting process $\{N(t),t \geq 0\}$ is said to be a Poisson process if it satisfies the following properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item $N(0)=0$
    \item The process $N(t)$ has independent increments.
      \item The process $N(t)$ has stationary increments.
\end{enumerate}
The pmf of a Poisson process is given as,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{P}(N(t)=n)=\frac{e^{-\lambda t}{(\lambda t)}^n}{n!}
    \label{eq:pmf}
\end{equation}
which models the number of arrivals in an interval $(0,t)$ and $\lambda$ is the Poisson rate. If we fix the value of the time variable $t$, then the values taken by $N(t)$ process is a random variable whose range is $\{0,1,2,3\dots\}$. The probability that the process takes a given value say $n$ is given by its pmf as shown in \eqref{eq:pmf}. 

The above given pmf satisfies the property $\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{e^{-\lambda t}{(\lambda t)}^n}{n!}$ =1 and thus it can be considered as a valid pmf.
\subsection*{Poisson Process: Properties}
The key properties possessed by a Poisson process that we studied in the previous lecture are :
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The sum of two Poisson processes is again a Poisson process with the rate parameter of the compound process as the sum of the rates of the individual processes.
    \item A Poisson process with rate parameter $\lambda$ can be split into individual queues with probabilities $p$ and $(1-p)$, then the arrivals at each of these queues can also be modeled using a Poisson process with rates $\lambda p$ and $\lambda(1-p)$
\end{enumerate}

The setting of $M/M/m/m$ queue is same as that of $M/M/m$ queue except that the buffer space/ the number of waiting positions in the queue is bounded. Here, since the system can hold a total of $m$ customers and there are $m$ servers, any arrival occurring when all the $m$ servers are busy will leave the system without waiting for service. This is called a $m$-\textit{channel loss system} and was first investigated by Erlang. $M/M/\infty$ queuing system is a queuing model where the customers arrivals follow a Poisson process with rate say, $\lambda$ and with infinite parallel servers having IID exponential service time distributions each with rate, say $\mu$. Since there are infinite servers, the arriving customers need not wait for their service and hence no queue is formed.
\subsection{M/G/1 Queue}
Consider a queue characterized by a Poisson arrival process. The service time may not always be exponential. For example, searching a database. It may even be deterministic. The assumption that service time is exponential is not always true. We now generalize M/M/1 queue for arbitrary characterization of service time. The service time (G) is still a random variable but with general distribution. 

PDF: $f_{X}(x)$
The PDF of service time may not be known. As long as we know $\overline{X},\overline{X^{2}}$, it is enough. Here $\overline{X}$ is the mean service time and $\overline{X^{2}}$ is the second moment of service time. $\mu$ is the service rate.

The so called Pollaczek-Khinchin Formula for the average waiting time of a customer in $M/G/1$ queue can be derived to be,
\begin{equation*}
    W = \frac{\lambda \overline{X^{2}}}{2(1-\rho)}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
    N_{Q} = \lambda W = \frac{\lambda^{2}\overline{X^{2}}}{2(1-\rho)}
\end{equation*}


We use little's theorem to find N.
\begin{equation*}
\begin{split}
        T &= W + \overline{X} \\
        &= \frac{\lambda \overline{X^{2}}}{2(1-\rho)} + \overline{X} \\
        N &= \lambda T = \frac{\lambda^{2} \overline{X^{2}}}{2(1-\rho)}\lambda\overline{X} \\
\end{split}
\end{equation*}

For a M/M/1 queue $\overline{X^{2}} = 2/\mu^{2}$, and so  $W = \rho/ \mu(1-\rho)$.

For a M/D/1 queue,$\overline{X^{2}} = 1/\mu^{2}$, and $W = \rho/ 2\mu(1-\rho)$.

\subsection{Renewal Theory}

It  a generic counting process it may be valid to assume that  the inter-arrival times between successive events are independent and identically distributed. However the inter-arrival times may not necessarily be exponentially distributed, it may have general distribution. 
Let $X_i$ be the inter-arrival time between two events.

$X_1,X_2,...$ need not be exponentially distributed. IN this case we have a counting process for which the times between successive events are independent and identically distributed with an arbitrary distribution. Such a counting process is called a renewal process. 
Let $N(t)$ denote the number of renewals that occur in time $t$. Let $\mu$ denote the rate at which renewals occur, then
with probability 1, $\frac{N(t)}{t} \to \frac{1}{\mu}$ as $t \to \infty$.
\subsection{Renewal Reward Process}
A large number of probability models are special cases of the following model. Consider a renewal process $\{N(t), t \geq 0\}$ having inter arrival times $\{X_{n},n \geq 1\}$, and suppose that each time a renewal occurs we receive a reward. We denote by $R_{n}$ the reward earned at the time of nth renewal. We shall assume that $R_{n}, n \geq 1$, are independent and identically distributed. However $R_{n}$ may depend on $X_{n}$, the length of the nth renewal arrival. 
\[R(t) = \sum_{n=1}^{N(t)}R_{n}\]
then R(t) represents the total reward earned by the time t. Let 
\[E[R] = E[R_{n}], E[X] = E[X_{n}]\] 
\textbf{Proposition}: If $E[R] < \infty \;\; and \;\; E[X] < \infty$, then 
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(a)] with probability 1, $      \lim_{t\to\infty} \frac{R(t)}{t} = \frac{E[R]}{E[X]}$
    \item[(b)]  $\lim_{t\to\infty} \frac{E[R(t)]}{t} = \frac{E[R]}{E[X]}$
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Proof.} We give the proof for (a). To prove this, 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{R(t)}{t} = \frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N(t)}R_{n}}{t} = \left(\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N(t)}R_{n}}{N(t)}\right)\left(\frac{N(t)}{t}\right)
\end{equation*}
By strong law of large numbers we obtain 
\[\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N(t)}R_{n}}{N(t)} \to E[R] \;\; as \;\; t\to \infty\] 
and by proposition 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{N(t)}{t} \to \frac{1}{E[X]} \;\; as \;\; t \to \infty
\end{equation*} 
If we say that a cycle is completed every time a renewal occurs, then the above proposition states that the long run average reward per unit time is equal to the expected reward earned during a cycle divided by the expected length of a cycle. \newline
Although we have supposed that the reward is earned at the time of a renewal, the result remains valid when the reward is earned gradually throughout the renewal cycle. 
\subsection{Example: Throughput of CSMA/CD }
Carrier sense multiple access with collision detection ( CSMA/CD )  is a popular medium access protocol for wireless networks. Consider a network with large number of nodes contending for the channel. Time is virtually slotted into mini slots. To avoid collision nodes keep sensing the channel and will transmit only at the end of an idle mini slot. Each node has a capability to sense channel as well as detect the collision of its packet. Each node will sense the mini slot and if the channel is found to be 
idle, it will transmit at the beginning of the next mini slot with probability "p". The duration for transmission of a packet is integral multiple of the length of mini slots. 
Let $\sigma$, denote the length of the mini slot.
The transmission duration of a packet is a  RV denoted as $L$ in units of $\sigma$.

Following things can happen when nodes attempt transmission .
\begin{itemize}
\item If no node transmit in that slot, the slot becomes idle, and all nodes sense an idle slot and make an attempt at the beginning of the next mini slot with probability "p".

\item If two or more nodes transmit, a collision occurs. Nodes involved in the collision can detect collision and stop transmission within that mini slot itself. This slot will be followed by one Idle slot for nodes to sense idleness before attempting transmission again.
\item If exactly one node transmit, then after an idle slot following the end of the transmission another attempt can begin.
\end{itemize}

We can model CSMA/CD as a renewal reward process to calculate the throughput. Throughput is defined as the expected number of packets successfully transmitted per unit time.

We can say that at the end of every idle mini slot, nodes can attempt transmission and these are the renewal points. The duration between successive renewals is 
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\sigma$ if no node transmits ( Idle case )
    \item $ 2 \sigma$ if more than one node transmit ( collision case )
    \item $E[L] + \sigma $ if exactly one node transmits ( success case ).
\end{itemize}

Let $P_{s}$ and $P_{c}$ be the probability of a successful transmission and collision to take place respectively.

Then expected duration of a renewal cycle is 
\begin{equation}
    \sigma ( 1- P_{s} + P_{c}  )   +  2 \sigma P_{c} + ( E[L] + \sigma )  P_{s}
\end{equation}
The  reward is the number of packets transmitted successfully in a renewal cycle. Expected reward is

1 * $P_{s}$ + 0 * ( 1- $P_{s}$) = $P_{s}$.
Therefore throughput $S$ is \begin{equation}
    S = \frac{P_{s}}{\sigma ( 1- P_{s} + P_{c}  )   +  2 \sigma P_{c} + ( E[L] + \sigma )  P_{s}}
\end{equation}

\section{Fixed-Point Analysis in MAC Modeling}
In the performance evaluation of distributed random-access protocols (such as IEEE 802.11 DCF), the interactions among contending nodes result in complex, coupled stochastic processes. Fixed-point analysis provides an elegant decoupling technique to determine the steady-state operating point of the network.

Under this framework, each station is modeled as an independent entity whose transmission probability in a generic slot, $\tau$, depends on the conditional collision probability $p$ that it experiences:
\begin{equation}
    \tau = f(p)
\end{equation}
Conversely, assuming a symmetric network of $n$ contending stations where each station transmits independently with probability $\tau$, the conditional collision probability seen by any given transmitting station is the probability that at least one of the remaining $n-1$ stations transmits in the same slot:
\begin{equation}
    p = g(\tau) = 1 - (1 - \tau)^{n-1}
\end{equation}
The steady-state behavior of the network is thus governed by the non-linear fixed-point equation:
\begin{equation}
    p = g(f(p)) \quad \text{or} \quad \tau = f(g(\tau))
\end{equation}
Existence and uniqueness of the operating point are established using fixed-point theorems (such as Brouwer's or Banach's contraction mapping theorem), while numerical values are computed via iterative substitution until convergence:
\begin{equation}
    p^{(k+1)} = g(f(p^{(k)}))
\end{equation}
Once the fixed-point $(\tau^*, p^*)$ is determined, key performance metrics such as saturation throughput, slot probabilities, and average access delay can be evaluated in closed form.

\section{Fluid Limit Approach}
As network scale increases---such as in ultra-dense WLANs, massive IoT deployments, or multi-user OFDMA systems with fine-grained resource allocations---the state space of discrete Markov chains grows exponentially, making exact stochastic analysis intractable. The fluid limit approach (or mean-field approximation) addresses this challenge by approximating discrete, stochastic packet and queue dynamics with continuous, deterministic trajectories.

Let $X^{(N)}(t)$ represent a vector of stochastic state variables (such as queue lengths or node states) in a system of scale $N$. By scaling space and time appropriately, the normalized state process is defined as:
\begin{equation}
    x^{(N)}(t) = \frac{X^{(N)}(t)}{N}
\end{equation}
Under standard regularity and Lipschitz conditions (governed by Kurtz's functional law of large numbers), as the system scale $N \to \infty$, the sample paths of the stochastic process $x^{(N)}(t)$ converge almost surely over finite time intervals to the deterministic trajectory $x(t)$ governed by a set of ordinary differential equations (ODEs):
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d x(t)}{dt} = F(x(t))
\end{equation}
where $F(x)$ represents the expected drift vector of the system given state $x$. 

The equilibrium of the fluid model, characterized by $F(x^*) = 0$, reveals the asymptotic steady-state behavior, queue stability conditions, and resource allocation efficiency without requiring the explicit generation or solution of massive transition rate matrices.

\section{Stochastic Geometry for Wireless Networks}
Traditional analytical models often assume simplified geometric topologies (such as grid networks or fixed transmission ranges). In contrast, stochastic geometry provides a rigorous mathematical framework for modeling the spatial randomness inherent in modern wireless deployments, where node locations (base stations, APs, and user equipments) fluctuate unpredictably across space.

\subsection{Spatial Poisson Point Processes}
The cornerstone of stochastic geometry is the Poisson Point Process (PPP). A homogeneous spatial Poisson Point Process $\Phi = \{x_i\} \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ of intensity $\lambda > 0$ satisfies two fundamental properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The number of nodes $\Phi(B)$ lying inside any bounded Borel set $B \subset \mathbb{R}^d$ is a Poisson random variable with parameter $\lambda |B|$:
    \begin{equation}
        P(\Phi(B) = k) = \frac{(\lambda |B|)^k}{k!} e^{-\lambda |B|}
    \end{equation}
    where $|B|$ denotes the $d$-dimensional Lebesgue measure (area/volume) of $B$.
    \item For disjoint regions $B_1, B_2, \dots, B_m$, the counts $\Phi(B_1), \dots, \Phi(B_m)$ are mutually independent random variables.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Campbell's Theorem and Probability Generating Functionals}
To evaluate aggregate network quantities (such as total received interference), stochastic geometry utilizes Campbell's theorem and the Probability Generating Functional (PGFL). For any non-negative measurable function $f(x)$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{x \in \Phi} f(x)\right] = \lambda \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} f(x) \, dx
\end{equation}
Furthermore, the PGFL of a homogeneous PPP $\Phi$ for a function $v(x) \in [0, 1]$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{E}\left[\prod_{x \in \Phi} v(x)\right] = \exp\left(-\lambda \int_{\mathbb{R}^d} (1 - v(x)) \, dx\right)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Interference and Coverage Probability}
Consider a typical receiver located at the origin. If transmitters in $\Phi \setminus \{x_0\}$ transmit with power $P$ over a path-loss channel $l(x) = \|x\|^{-\alpha}$ (with path-loss exponent $\alpha > 2$) and Rayleigh fading channels with power gains $h_i \sim \text{Exp}(1)$, the aggregate interference $I$ is:
\begin{equation}
    I = \sum_{x_i \in \Phi \setminus \{x_0\}} P h_i \|x_i\|^{-\alpha}
\end{equation}
The Laplace transform of the interference, $\mathcal{L}_I(s) = \mathbb{E}[e^{-sI}]$, can be evaluated in closed form using the PGFL:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_I(s) = \exp\left(-\pi \lambda s^{2/\alpha} \Gamma(1 - 2/\alpha) \mathbb{E}[h^{2/\alpha}]\right)
\end{equation}

\label{sec:chapter_wlan}

It focuses on fundamental channel access mechanisms, mathematical throughput and jitter modeling, Quality of Service (QoS) differentiation, millimeter-wave architectures, and cross-layer physical enhancements including Multi-Packet Reception (MPR), Successive Interference Cancellation (SIC), and Full-Duplex communication.

\section{Architecture and Medium Access Fundamentals}
The IEEE 802.11 WLAN architecture defines two fundamental deployment configurations: the \textbf{Basic Service Set (BSS)}, in which all client stations communicate through a centralized Access Point (AP), and the \textbf{Independent Basic Service Set (IBSS)}, in which stations communicate directly in an ad-hoc, decentralized manner without an infrastructure coordinator.

\subsection{Why Not CSMA/CD?}
In wired Ethernet networks (IEEE 802.3), Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Detection (CSMA/CD) is widely deployed. However, CSMA/CD cannot be directly implemented in wireless environments due to three physical constraints:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Half-Duplex Radio Limitation:} Wireless transceivers typically transmit and receive on the same frequency channel. A station's own high transmit power severely drowns out any incoming signals, rendering concurrent collision detection impossible during active transmission.
    \item \textbf{Rapid Signal Attenuation:} Due to path loss and shadowing, received signal power decays steeply over distance ($d^{-\alpha}$). A collision occurring near a remote receiver might produce signal fluctuations too faint to be detected at the transmitter.
    \item \textbf{Hidden Station Problem:} Collisions occur at the receiver rather than at the sender. Two transmitting stations out of mutual sensing range may transmit simultaneously to a common receiver, producing overlapping packets that cannot be sensed at the transmitter ends.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{CSMA with Collision Avoidance (CSMA/CA)}
To overcome the inability to detect collisions during transmission, IEEE 802.11 adopts Carrier Sense Multiple Access with Collision Avoidance (CSMA/CA). Instead of detecting collisions after they happen, CSMA/CA minimizes collision probability proactively:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Physical Carrier Sensing:} Stations perform clear channel assessment (CCA) by sensing RF energy and detecting preamble preambles before initiating transmission.
    \item \textbf{Virtual Carrier Sensing:} Stations maintain a timer called the Network Allocation Vector (NAV), populated from the Duration fields in overheard frame headers. The medium is treated as busy as long as the NAV is non-zero.
    \item \textbf{Inter-Frame Spacings (IFS):} Strict priority ordering is enforced through geometric time intervals, including Short Inter-Frame Space (SIFS), Priority Inter-Frame Space (PIFS), and Distributed Inter-Frame Space (DIFS), satisfying $\text{SIFS} < \text{PIFS} < \text{DIFS}$.
    \item \textbf{Random Slotted Backoff:} If the medium is sensed busy, stations defer and initialize a backoff counter uniformly distributed in the contention window $[0, CW - 1]$. The counter decrements by one for each idle slot time $\sigma$, freezes whenever the medium is busy, and resumes once the medium has remained idle for a full DIFS duration.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{RTS/CTS Handshake and Exposed Station Resolution}
To address the hidden station problem, IEEE 802.11 provides a four-way handshake mechanism:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RTS/CTS Handshake:} The transmitter sends a short Request-to-Send (RTS) frame. The destination replies with a Clear-to-Send (CTS) frame after SIFS. Surrounding nodes hearing either RTS or CTS update their NAV for the entire transaction duration, preventing hidden terminal collisions during the longer data payload.
    \item \textbf{Exposed Station Problem:} Occurs when a station refrains from transmitting to an independent receiver because it overhears a nearby transmitter, even though its transmission would not cause interference at the existing receiver. Virtual carrier sensing and directional beamforming are primary mechanisms used to mitigate this issue.
\end{itemize}

\section{IEEE 802.11 DCF Performance Modeling}
The Distributed Coordination Function (DCF) forms the foundation of contention-based channel access in IEEE 802.11.

\subsection{Bianchi's 2-D Markov Chain Model for Saturated DCF}
To quantify the theoretical saturation throughput of IEEE 802.11 DCF, Bianchi formulated a discrete-time two-dimensional Markov chain $\{s(t), b(t)\}$, where $s(t) \in [0, m]$ represents the backoff stage at slot time $t$, and $b(t) \in [0, W_i - 1]$ denotes the backoff counter, with $W_i = 2^i W_0$ being the contention window at stage $i$.

Assuming constant conditional collision probability $p$ in each transmission attempt, the station transmission probability $\tau$ is derived as:
\begin{equation}
    \tau = \sum_{i=0}^m b_{i,0} = \frac{2(1 - 2p)}{(1 - 2p)(W_0 + 1) + p W_0 (1 - (2p)^m)}
\end{equation}
For a network of $n$ independent, contending stations, the conditional collision probability $p$ is the probability that at least one of the remaining $n - 1$ stations transmits in the same slot:
\begin{equation}
    p = 1 - (1 - \tau)^{n - 1}
\end{equation}
The unique operating point $(\tau^*, p^*)$ is obtained via fixed-point analysis. Letting $P_{tr} = 1 - (1 - \tau)^n$ denote the probability that at least one station transmits in a generic slot, and $P_s = \frac{n \tau (1 - \tau)^{n-1}}{P_{tr}}$ denote the conditional probability of a successful transmission, the normalized saturation throughput $S$ is:
\begin{equation}
    S = \frac{P_{tr} P_s E[P]}{(1 - P_{tr})\sigma + P_{tr} P_s T_s + P_{tr} (1 - P_s) T_c}
\end{equation}
where $E[P]$ is the expected payload length, $T_s$ is the average channel duration of a successful transmission, and $T_c$ is the average duration of a collision.

\subsection{Unsaturated Traffic Conditions and Queue State Modeling}
In practical networks, client buffers are frequently empty (unsaturated traffic conditions). The saturated model is generalized by coupling the discrete-time backoff Markov chain with a single-server queueing model. 

When a station successfully transmits or drops a packet and its queue is empty, it transitions to an idle state with probability $1 - q$, where $q$ is the probability that at least one packet is present in the buffer at the beginning of a slot. The transmission probability under unsaturated conditions scales proportionally with queue occupancy:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{unsat} = q \cdot \tau_{sat}
\end{equation}
Analytical results demonstrate that below a critical arrival rate $\lambda_c$, the network operates in a stable, low-contention regime where collisions are minimal. As arrival rates exceed $\lambda_c$, packet queues saturate, causing the network to transition abruptly into the contention-limited saturated regime.

\subsection{Packet Delay and Delay Jitter Analysis}
For real-time multimedia services, access delay and packet delay variance (jitter) are critical Quality of Service metrics. Let $D$ be the random variable denoting the access delay from the instant a frame reaches the head of the queue until its successful reception.

The access delay consists of the accumulated backoff intervals and transmission attempts across successive backoff stages:
\begin{equation}
    D = \sum_{j=0}^{k} \left( \sum_{l=1}^{B_j} T_{slot, l} + T_{attempt, j} \right)
\end{equation}
where $k$ is the number of retransmissions experienced, $B_j$ is the uniform random backoff counter at stage $j$, and $T_{slot}$ is the random duration of a backoff slot.

The delay jitter, defined as the standard deviation or variance of the frame access delay, is derived from the second moment of service time:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Var}[D] = \mathbb{E}[D^2] - (\mathbb{E}[D])^2
\end{equation}
Closed-form expressions for $\mathrm{Var}[D]$ confirm that while increasing the initial contention window $W_0$ reduces collision probability, it increases delay jitter, revealing a fundamental tradeoff between collision mitigation and bounded latency variance.

\section{Quality of Service and IEEE 802.11e EDCA}
The legacy IEEE 802.11 standard treats all traffic categories identically. To guarantee differentiated Quality of Service for voice, video, and best-effort applications, IEEE 802.11e introduced the \textbf{Enhanced Distributed Channel Access (EDCA)} mechanism within the Hybrid Coordination Function (HCF).

\subsection{Access Categories and Parameter Differentiation}
In an EDCA station, the MAC layer maintains four parallel queues corresponding to four Access Categories (ACs):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{AC\_VO (Voice):} Highest priority, designed for conversational real-time traffic.
    \item \textbf{AC\_VI (Video):} High priority, prioritized for streaming multimedia.
    \item \textbf{AC\_BE (Best Effort):} Medium priority, standard data traffic (e.g., HTTP, FTP).
    \item \textbf{AC\_BK (Background):} Lowest priority, bulk data and background print traffic.
\end{enumerate}

Service differentiation is achieved through specific parameter sets per AC:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Arbitration Inter-Frame Space (AIFS):} The duration the medium must remain idle before backoff commences:
    \begin{equation}
        AIFS[AC] = SIFS + AIFSN[AC] \times \sigma
    \end{equation}
    \item \textbf{Contention Window Bounds ($CW_{min}[AC], CW_{max}[AC]$):} High-priority ACs utilize significantly smaller contention windows to secure statistical access priority.
    \item \textbf{Transmission Opportunity (TXOP):} The maximum duration a station is granted channel ownership to burst multiple frames separated by SIFS upon winning contention.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Internal and External Collision Resolution}
In EDCA, two distinct collision mechanisms occur:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Internal (Virtual) Collisions:} Occur when two or more internal AC queues within the same physical station decrement their backoff counters to zero simultaneously. The internal scheduler resolves this collision by granting the transmission opportunity to the highest-priority AC, while the lower-priority ACs double their contention windows and re-enter backoff as if an external collision occurred.
    \item \textbf{External Collisions:} Occur when two different stations transmit on the wireless medium simultaneously.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analytical Modeling of EDCA with Virtual Collisions and Finite Retrials}
Following the analytical framework developed by Sitaram et al.~\cite{sitaram80211e}, we model the four Access Categories $\alpha \in \{0, 1, 2, 3\}$ ($\mathrm{AC}_0$: Voice, $\mathrm{AC}_1$: Video, $\mathrm{AC}_2$: Best Effort, $\mathrm{AC}_3$: Background) operating under saturated traffic conditions. The model incorporates:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Internal (Virtual) Collisions:} Simultaneous countdown expiry among local queues within the station. The internal scheduler grants channel access to the highest-priority contending AC (lowest index $\alpha$), while lower-priority ACs double their contention windows and retry as if an external collision occurred.
    \item \textbf{Differentiated Access Parameters:} Each AC is parameterized by contention window bounds $[W_0^\alpha, W_{m_\alpha}^\alpha]$, retry limit $f_\alpha$, arbitration space $\mathrm{AIFS}_\alpha = \mathrm{SIFS} + \mathrm{AIFSN}_\alpha \times \sigma$, and transmission opportunity bursting $L_\alpha = \lfloor \frac{\mathrm{TXOP}_\alpha}{\text{packet length}} \rfloor$ frames ($L_0, L_1 \ge 1$; $L_2 = L_3 = 1$).
    \item \textbf{External Collisions:} Concurrent transmissions across $n$ independent stations sharing the wireless medium.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Conditional Collision Probabilities}
The internal collision probability $P_{I\alpha}$ experienced by $\mathrm{AC}_\alpha$ is governed by the transmission events of strictly higher-priority ACs in the same node:
\begin{align}
    P_{I0} &= 0, \\
    P_{I1} &= \tau_0, \\
    P_{I2} &= 1 - (1 - \tau_0)(1 - \tau_1), \\
    P_{I3} &= 1 - (1 - \tau_0)(1 - \tau_1)(1 - \tau_2).
\end{align}
Letting $P_{ex} = 1 - (1 - \tau)^{n-1}$ denote the external collision probability from the remaining $n-1$ contending stations (where $\tau = 1 - \prod_{\alpha=0}^3 (1 - \tau_\alpha)$ is the aggregate transmission probability of a station in a generic slot), the total conditional collision probability for $\mathrm{AC}_\alpha$ is:
\begin{equation}
    P_\alpha = P_{I\alpha} + (1 - P_{I\alpha}) P_{ex}.
\end{equation}

The conditional success probability $P_{S\alpha}$ given that a transmission occurs on the channel is:
\begin{align}
    P_{S0} &= \frac{n \tau_0 (1 - \tau)^{n-1}}{1 - (1 - \tau)^n}, \\
    P_{S1} &= \frac{n \tau_1 (1 - \tau_0)(1 - \tau)^{n-1}}{1 - (1 - \tau)^n}, \\
    P_{S2} &= \frac{n \tau_2 (1 - \tau_0)(1 - \tau_1)(1 - \tau)^{n-1}}{1 - (1 - \tau)^n}, \\
    P_{S3} &= \frac{n \tau_3 (1 - \tau_0)(1 - \tau_1)(1 - \tau_2)(1 - \tau)^{n-1}}{1 - (1 - \tau)^n}.
\end{align}

\subsubsection{Station Transmission Probability $\tau_\alpha$}
For each $\mathrm{AC}_\alpha$, the discrete-time Markov chain transition structure with $m_\alpha$ window doubling stages, $f_\alpha$ maximum window retry stages, and $L_\alpha$ burst transmission states yields the stationary probability of the initial state $b_{0,0}^\alpha$:
\begin{equation}
    b_{0,0}^\alpha = \left[ L_\alpha W_0^\alpha + \frac{1 - (2P_\alpha)^{m_\alpha+1}}{1 - 2P_\alpha}\frac{W_0^\alpha}{2} + \frac{1 - P_\alpha^{m_\alpha+1}}{1 - P_\alpha}\frac{1}{2} + P_\alpha^{m_\alpha+1}\frac{W_{m_\alpha}^\alpha}{2}\frac{1 - P_\alpha^{f_\alpha}}{1 - P_\alpha} \right]^{-1}.
\end{equation}
The transmission probability of $\mathrm{AC}_\alpha$ in a generic slot is then:
\begin{equation}
    \tau_\alpha = \frac{1 - P_\alpha^{m_\alpha + f_\alpha + 1}}{1 - P_\alpha} b_{0,0}^\alpha.
\end{equation}
The simultaneous non-linear system of equations is solved numerically to obtain the unique operating point $(\tau_\alpha, P_\alpha)$.

\subsubsection{Average Frame Transmission Delay and Jitter}
The average frame transmission delay $E[D_\alpha]$ for $\mathrm{AC}_\alpha$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    E[D_\alpha] = \frac{E[N_\alpha] \cdot E[T_\alpha]}{L_\alpha},
\end{equation}
where $E[N_\alpha]$ is the expected number of backoff slot decrements required for successful frame delivery:
\begin{equation}
    E[N_\alpha] = \left[ \frac{1 - (2P_\alpha)^{m_\alpha+1}}{1 - 2P_\alpha}\frac{W_0^\alpha}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\frac{1 - P_\alpha^{m_\alpha+1}}{1 - P_\alpha} + \frac{W_{m_\alpha}^\alpha}{2} P_\alpha^{m_\alpha+1}\frac{1 - P_\alpha^{f_\alpha}}{1 - P_\alpha} \right] \frac{1 - P_\alpha}{1 - P_\alpha^{m_\alpha + f_\alpha + 1}},
\end{equation}
and $E[T_\alpha]$ is the expected duration of a generic slot transition:
\begin{equation}
    E[T_\alpha] = (1 - P_{tr})\sigma + P_{tr} \left[ (\mathrm{AIFS}_\alpha - \mathrm{AIFS}_{\min}) + \sum_{\beta=0}^3 P_{S\beta} T_s^\beta + \sum_{\beta=0}^3 (1 - P_{S\beta}) T_c^\beta \right].
\end{equation}
Here $P_{tr} = 1 - (1 - \tau)^n$, and $T_s^\beta, T_c^\beta$ are the durations of successful transmissions and collisions respectively.

The delay jitter $J_\alpha$, defined as the square root of delay variance $\mathrm{Var}[D_\alpha]$, evaluates in closed form to:
\begin{equation}
    J_\alpha = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[D_\alpha]} = \frac{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[N_\alpha]} \cdot E[T_\alpha]}{L_\alpha},
\end{equation}
with:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Var}[N_\alpha] = \sum_{i=0}^{m_\alpha + f_\alpha} \left[ \frac{(W_i^\alpha)^2 - 1}{12} + \left( \frac{W_i^\alpha - 1}{2} \right)^2 \right] \frac{P_\alpha^i (1 - P_\alpha)}{1 - P_\alpha^{m_\alpha + f_\alpha + 1}} - (E[N_\alpha])^2.
\end{equation}
Analytical computations demonstrate that smaller contention windows, reduced AIFSN, TXOP burst transmission, and prioritized virtual collision resolution jointly guarantee significantly lower delay and jitter for voice and video streams, insulating them from lower-priority best-effort traffic under saturated conditions~\cite{sitaram80211e}.


\section{IEEE 802.11ad 60 GHz Directional MAC Architecture}
The availability of massive unlicensed spectrum in the 60 GHz millimeter-wave band enabled the IEEE 802.11ad standard to achieve multi-gigabit data rates (exceeding 1 Gbps).

\subsection{Millimeter-Wave Channel Characteristics}
Communication at 60 GHz offers multi-gigahertz channels but encounters severe propagation challenges:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Oxygen Absorption Peak:} Oxygen molecules absorb RF energy prominently at 60 GHz, introducing severe path loss (approximately 16 dB/km).
    \item \textbf{Blockage Vulnerability:} The small millimeter wavelength ($\lambda \approx 5$ mm) prevents penetration through walls and human bodies, confining links to line-of-sight (LOS) indoor environments.
    \item \textbf{Directional Antenna Arrays:} To overcome attenuation, 60 GHz devices employ highly directional phased-array beamforming antennas.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{PBSS Architecture and Beacon Interval Structure}
IEEE 802.11ad adopts a \textbf{Personal Basic Service Set (PBSS)} architecture, coordinated by a PBSS Central Point (PCP) or Access Point (AP). Channel access is organized into periodic \textbf{Beacon Intervals (BIs)}. The PCP/AP coordinates channel access through beacon frames, generating schedules and communicating them to nodes using beacon and announcement frames.

The BI is divided into two main sub-intervals:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Beacon Header Interval (BHI):} Consists of three access periods:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Beacon Transmission Interval (BTI):} The PCP/AP transmits directional DMG beacon frames to establish network synchronization across multiple antenna sectors.
        \item \textbf{Association Beamforming Training (A-BFT):} Client stations perform beamforming training with the PCP/AP.
        \item \textbf{Announcement Transmission Interval (ATI):} A request-response based management access period between the PCP/AP and associated nodes. During the ATI, the PCP/AP polls nodes. Associated nodes requiring service periods respond with a Service Period Request (SPR). Based on these requests and QoS requirements, the PCP/AP allocates SPs in subsequent frames, tracking outstanding requests.
    \end{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Data Transmission Interval (DTI):}
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Contention-Based Access Periods (CBAP):} Contention access using slotted CSMA/CA.
        \item \textbf{Service Periods (SPs):} Dedicated, scheduled time slots allocated exclusively to specific transmitter-receiver pairs for QoS data exchange and power management.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Queuing Modeling of Scheduled Service Periods (PGQS)}
Analytical studies by Hemanth and Venkatesh~\cite{hemanth2014iet, hemanth2019tett} model the scheduled Service Periods (SPs) of the IEEE 802.11ad hybrid MAC as a special case of a \textbf{Periodically Gated Queueing System (PGQS)} with Bernoulli scheduling. During non-SP intervals (which include BTI, A-BFT, ATI, and CBAPs or other nodes' SPs), the queue is gated (in vacation), accumulating Poisson arrivals with rate $\lambda$. When the dedicated SP opens, queued packets are served sequentially with packet transmission time $L$.

\subsubsection{PGF of Queue Length and Waiting Time}
Let $X$ denote the duration of the vacation period between successive SP visits. In a stable PGQS with Bernoulli scheduling parameter $q$, the probability generating function (PGF) of the queue length $Q_a(z)$ at an arbitrary epoch during the vacation is related to the queue length distributions at vacation start and end epochs:
\begin{equation}
    Q_a(z) = \frac{1 - V(z)}{\lambda E[X](1 - z)} Q_v(z),
\end{equation}
where $V(z) = X^*(\lambda(1 - z))$ is the PGF of arrivals during vacation duration $X$, and $Q_v(z)$ is the PGF of customer queue lengths at vacation-ending epochs~\cite{hemanth2019tett}.

Applying Brumelle's theorem, the factorial moments of queue length map directly to the moments of waiting time $W$:
\begin{equation}
    E[W] = \frac{E[Q_a]}{\lambda}, \qquad E[W^2] = \frac{1}{\lambda^2} \left. \frac{d^2 Q_a(z)}{dz^2} \right|_{z=1^-}.
\end{equation}
The variance of delay in the PGQS stage is therefore:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{Var}[W] = E[W^2] - (E[W])^2.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Overall Delay and Delay Jitter (PDV)}
A packet's end-to-end latency consists of the reservation and scheduling delay in PGQS ($W$) plus the transmission delay in the final Beacon Interval ($D_i$) where the tagged station occupies the $i$-th service period order:
\begin{equation}
    E[D_i] = (i - 1)\rho L + (N - i)L_s + L,
\end{equation}
where $\rho$ is the probability of SP reservation, $L_s$ is the sensing/IFS overhead, and $N$ is the number of associated stations. Averaging over all positions $i \in \{1, \dots, N\}$, the mean end-to-end delay is derived in closed form~\cite{hemanth2019tett}:
\begin{equation}
    E[\text{Delay}] = \frac{E[Q_a]}{\lambda} + \frac{N - 1}{2}\left[ \frac{\lambda(B + \alpha)L}{q - N\lambda L} + L_s \right] + L,
\end{equation}
where $B$ is the Beacon Interval duration, $\alpha$ is the gating overhead, and $q$ is the Bernoulli SP availability probability.

Assuming stochastic independence between queueing vacation delays and intra-BI transmission scheduling, the packet delay variation (PDV or delay jitter) is:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PDV} = \sqrt{\mathrm{Var}[W] + \mathrm{Var}[D_i]}.
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{Stability Condition and Admission Control}
The closed-form delay expression reveals that the system remains stable as long as the arrival rate is strictly below the critical threshold:
\begin{equation}
    \lambda^* = \frac{q}{N L}.
\end{equation}
As $\lambda \to \lambda^*$, queue lengths grow asymptotically and packet delay becomes unbounded. This critical arrival rate provides a rigorous closed-form criterion for admission control and dynamic SP duration allocation in IEEE 802.11ad millimeter-wave networks~\cite{hemanth2014iet, hemanth2019tett}.

\section{Physical Layer Enhancements: Multi-Packet Reception (MPR) MAC}
Advancements in the physical layer---such as Multi-User MIMO (MU-MIMO) and Successive Interference Cancellation (SIC)---enable wireless receivers to decode multiple overlapping packet transmissions simultaneously, known as \textbf{Multi-Packet Reception (MPR)}.

\subsection{Order Statistics Analysis of Pure ALOHA under MPR}
Under a $K$-MPR channel model, up to $K$ concurrent packet transmissions can be decoded without error. Using order statistics, the arrival times of $N$ packets arriving within a frame window are evaluated. The probability of capturing $j \le K$ packets is obtained in closed form from the joint distribution of arrival order statistics, demonstrating significant throughput gains over standard single-packet capture ALOHA.

\subsection{Analytical Modeling of CSMA under MPR}
Balan and Venkatesh \cite{balan2015analysis} developed an exact throughput analysis for unslotted carrier sense multiple access protocols under a $K$-MPR channel model. In this model, up to $K$ overlapping packets can be successfully received. If the number of concurrent transmissions exceeds $K$, all packets in the slot fail.

For an unslotted Non-Persistent CSMA (NP-CSMA) protocol, the average duration of the idle time is $1/\Lambda$, where $\Lambda$ is the Poisson arrival rate of new and rescheduled packets. The channel timeline alternates between idle and busy periods. A busy period is initiated by the first packet arrival at some time $t$. Any subsequent packets that arrive within the carrier sensing delay $a$ (representing the signal propagation delay) will also be transmitted immediately because the busy channel state has not yet been sensed by those nodes. The interval $[t, t+a]$ is the vulnerable interval.

Let $Y$ be the random variable denoting the duration between the start of the first transmission ($t$) and the last arrival in the vulnerable interval $[t, t+a]$. The cumulative distribution function (CDF) of $Y$ is given by:
\begin{equation}
    F_Y(y) = e^{-\Lambda(a - y)}, \quad 0 \le y \le a
\end{equation}
The mean busy duration $\bar{B}$ (with packet duration normalized to 1) is:
\begin{equation}
    \bar{B} = 1 + 2a - \frac{1}{\Lambda}(1 - e^{-a\Lambda})
\end{equation}
Under $K$-MPR, all transmissions are successful if and only if the total number of packets transmitted during the busy interval is at most $K$. Applying the renewal reward theorem, the normalized system throughput $S$ of NP-CSMA under MPR is:
\begin{equation}
    S = \frac{\Lambda e^{-\Lambda a} \sum_{i=0}^{K-1} \frac{(i+1)(\Lambda a)^i}{i!}}{1 + (2a + 1)\Lambda - (1 - e^{-a\Lambda})}
\end{equation}

Extending the adaptive backoff philosophy to QoS networks, Adaptive EDCA dynamically adjusts the decrement step size $d_{AC}(i)$ according to the traffic Access Category. \section{IEEE 802.11ax Multi-User Enhancements}
IEEE 802.11ax represents a major milestone in WLAN evolution, designed to achieve at least four times higher average per-station (STA) throughput in dense deployments compared to legacy standards. To address dynamic scenarios where STAs frequently join and leave the network while supporting diverse QoS requirements, the standard introduces key Physical (PHY) and Medium Access Control (MAC) layer enhancements.

\subsection{OBSS Interference Mitigation and Frequency Reuse}
In high-density scenarios (e.g., smart homes, enterprise environments, and auditoriums), numerous Access Points (APs) are deployed in close proximity, causing severe Overlapping Basic Service Set (OBSS) interference. To mitigate this interference, the standard leverages:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Frequency Reuse:} Operating across the 2.4~GHz and 5~GHz ISM bands (and extending to 6~GHz in Wi-Fi 6E). While 2.4~GHz provides up to 3 non-overlapping 20~MHz channels, 5~GHz supports up to 23 non-overlapping channels, mitigating co-channel interference at the cost of higher path loss through physical obstacles.
    \item \textbf{MU-OFDMA Resource Units:} Subdividing channel bandwidth into orthogonal subcarriers grouped into Resource Units (RUs) of varying sizes (e.g., RU-26, RU-52, RU-106, RU-242).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hybrid Access (HA) Methodology}
\label{sec:ha_proc}
In scheduled access (SA) mechanisms, RUs are planned and assigned deterministically by the Access Point (AP), eliminating collisions and maximizing throughput. However, centralized SA requires the AP to periodically poll uplink requirements, incurring signaling overhead and latency. In contrast, pure random access (RA) achieves low access delay for newly joined STAs at the expense of collision-induced throughput degradation under high load.

To achieve an optimal operating balance, IEEE 802.11ax implements a \textbf{Hybrid Access (HA)} mechanism integrating scheduled access and distributed random access:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The AP broadcasts RU availability and allocation parameters using a Trigger Frame-Request (TF-R) frame.
    \item Contending STAs decrement their backoff counters by the number of random access RUs ($N_{\text{RA}}$). When a counter reaches zero, the STA transmits its Buffer Status Report (BSR) on a randomly selected RA RU.
    \item The state of each RA RU is classified into:
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \emph{Success:} Exactly one STA transmits its BSR on the RU.
        \item \emph{Collision/Failure:} Two or more STAs transmit simultaneously on the RU.
        \item \emph{Idle:} No STA selects the RU.
    \end{itemize}
    \item The AP decodes successful BSRs and schedules uplink data transmissions via a Trigger Frame (TF). Scheduled STAs then transmit aggregated payloads (A-MPDUs), which are acknowledged by the AP using a Multi-Station Block Acknowledgement (MS-BACK) frame.
\end{enumerate}
The complete duration of this exchange cycle is given by:
\begin{equation}
    t_s = T_{\text{TF-R}} + 4 T_{\text{SIFS}} + T_{\text{BSR}} + T_{\text{TF}} + T_{\text{data}} + T_{\text{MS-BACK}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Packet Aggregation and Air-Time Efficiency}
\label{sec:aggr}
To maximize air-time efficiency in dense networks, scheduled STAs aggregate multiple MAC Protocol Data Units (MPDUs) into an Aggregate MPDU (A-MPDU) during their allocated TXOP duration ($T_{\text{data}}$). Each MPDU combines payload data with MAC headers and tail bits, sharing a single Physical Layer Convergence Procedure (PLCP) header ($T_{\text{PHY}}^{\text{he}}$), thereby substantially reducing control overhead.

\subsection{Multi-User MIMO and Channel Sounding}
In addition to frequency-domain multiplexing via MU-OFDMA, IEEE 802.11ax supports Multi-User MIMO (MU-MIMO) across RUs comprising 106 subcarriers or more. Using multi-antenna arrays (up to 8 antennas at the AP and 4 antennas at user STAs), the AP steers spatial beams to multiple STAs concurrently. To decode MU-MIMO transmissions, accurate Channel State Information (CSI) is maintained through periodic channel sounding, providing the spatial channel matrices required for beamforming precoding.

\section{Physical Layer Enhancements: SIC and Full-Duplex MAC}
Further physical-layer integrations include Successive Interference Cancellation (SIC) and in-band Full-Duplex (FD) communications.

\subsection{SIC-Based Slotted ALOHA with Signal Diversity}
Successive Interference Cancellation (SIC) allows a receiver to decode overlapping packets sequentially by decoding the strongest packet first, reconstructing its analog waveform, subtracting it from the composite signal, and decoding the remaining packets. 

Combining Slotted ALOHA with signal transmission diversity and SIC enables nodes to transmit multiple packet replicas across time slots. Collisions in earlier slots are iteratively resolved backwards once an uncollided replica is decoded in a subsequent slot, doubling the theoretical throughput limit of ALOHA.
