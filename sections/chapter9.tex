\chapter{Vehicular Networks}
\label{sec:chapter9}

Vehicular communication networks constitute an important component of
Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS), enabling vehicles to exchange
information with other vehicles, roadside infrastructure, pedestrians, and
communication networks. The integration of wireless communication with
on-board sensing and computing allows vehicles to obtain information beyond
the sensing range of their local sensors and supports applications ranging
from collision avoidance and traffic management to cooperative driving and
passenger infotainment.

Vehicular communication technology has evolved considerably over the past
two decades. Early research primarily focused on Vehicular Ad Hoc Networks
(VANETs), in which vehicles dynamically establish wireless links with nearby
vehicles and roadside infrastructure. Dedicated Short-Range Communications
(DSRC), based primarily on IEEE 802.11p and the Wireless Access in Vehicular
Environments (WAVE) architecture, emerged as one of the earliest standardized
solutions for vehicular communication
\cite{kenney2011dsrc,jiang2008ieee,morgan2010dsrc}.

The rapid development of cellular communication subsequently resulted in
Cellular Vehicle-to-Everything (C-V2X). LTE-V2X introduced direct sidelink
communication between vehicles through the PC5 interface, while the evolution
toward fifth-generation cellular systems resulted in New Radio V2X
(NR-V2X). NR-V2X extends the capabilities of LTE-V2X to support advanced
applications including vehicle platooning, cooperative perception, advanced
driving, and remote driving
\cite{seo2016lte,molinamasegosa2017ltev,lien2020nr}.

This chapter presents the evolution of vehicular networking technologies,
beginning with VANETs and IEEE 802.11p-based communication and subsequently
discussing LTE-V2X and NR-V2X. Particular emphasis is placed on safety
message dissemination, mobility-related challenges, sidelink communication,
and radio resource allocation.

\section{Vehicular Communication Networks}
\label{sec:vehicular_communication_networks}
% ============================================================

A vehicular network consists of vehicles equipped with communication,
sensing, positioning, and computing capabilities that exchange information
with other entities in the transportation environment. Unlike conventional
wireless networks, vehicular networks are characterized by high node
mobility, rapidly changing network topology, intermittent connectivity,
stringent communication latency requirements, and highly dynamic wireless
channels.


\begin{figure}[!t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]
    {figures/Chapter 9 images/TS-V2X-communications_W640.jpg}
    \caption{V2X communication architecture and communication modes.}
    \label{fig:v2x_communications}
\end{figure}

Vehicular communications are commonly grouped under the general term
Vehicle-to-Everything (V2X). Depending on the communicating entities, V2X
communication can be categorized as follows:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Vehicle-to-Vehicle (V2V):} Direct communication among
    vehicles for exchanging information such as position, velocity,
    acceleration, heading, and safety warnings.

    \item \textbf{Vehicle-to-Infrastructure (V2I):} Communication between
    vehicles and roadside infrastructure such as Roadside Units (RSUs),
    traffic lights, or traffic-management systems.

    \item \textbf{Vehicle-to-Pedestrian (V2P):} Communication between
    vehicles and vulnerable road users equipped with compatible communication
    devices.

    \item \textbf{Vehicle-to-Network (V2N):} Communication between vehicles
    and remote network services through cellular infrastructure.
\end{itemize}

Together, these communication modes provide vehicles with information beyond
their direct sensing range and enable cooperative decision making among road
users.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Evolution from VANETs to V2X Networks}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Vehicular Ad Hoc Networks evolved from Mobile Ad Hoc Networks (MANETs), but
the mobility characteristics of vehicles distinguish VANETs from conventional
ad hoc networks. Vehicle movement is constrained by road geometry and traffic
rules, while relative velocities can be very high. Consequently, communication
links may exist only for short durations and the network topology can change
rapidly.

Early VANET architectures relied mainly on distributed communication among
vehicles and RSUs. IEEE 802.11p was developed specifically to support
low-latency wireless access in vehicular environments
\cite{jiang2008ieee,kenney2011dsrc}. The resulting DSRC/WAVE architecture
enabled direct communication without requiring vehicles to first establish a
connection with cellular infrastructure.

The emergence of LTE and device-to-device communication introduced a second
major technology family for V2X. Cellular V2X combines the wide-area
connectivity of cellular networks with direct sidelink communication between
vehicles. The resulting evolution can broadly be represented as

\[
\text{VANET}
\rightarrow
\text{DSRC/IEEE 802.11p}
\rightarrow
\text{LTE-V2X}
\rightarrow
\text{NR-V2X}
\rightarrow
\text{Internet of Vehicles}.
\]

Future vehicular networks increasingly integrate communication, sensing,
computing, cloud services, and edge intelligence, leading toward the broader
concept of the Internet of Vehicles (IoV)
\cite{zhou2020evolutionary,xu2018iov}.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{V2X Applications}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Vehicular communication networks support a wide range of applications that
can broadly be classified into four categories: \emph{road safety},
\emph{traffic management}, \emph{infotainment}, and
\emph{autonomous driving}, as illustrated in
Fig.~\ref{fig:v2x_applications}.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=10cm, height=6cm]
    {figures/Chapter 9 images/V2X APPLICATIONS.png}
    \caption{Major application categories supported by V2X communication networks.}
    \label{fig:v2x_applications}
\end{figure}

\begin{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Road Safety:}
    Road-safety applications are among the most time-critical services in
    vehicular networks and generally require highly reliable and low-latency
    communication. Typical applications include collision warning, collision
    avoidance, road-hazard warning, intersection warning, remote diagnostics,
    and emergency-vehicle alerts.

    \item \textbf{Traffic Management:}
    Traffic-management applications utilize information exchanged among
    vehicles and roadside infrastructure to improve traffic flow and reduce
    congestion. Representative services include speed management, traffic
    information dissemination, routing information, and cooperative
    navigation.

    \item \textbf{Infotainment:}
    Infotainment applications provide connectivity and multimedia services to
    vehicle occupants. These services generally tolerate larger communication
    delays than safety applications but may require higher data rates.
    Examples include in-vehicle Internet access, video streaming, online
    gaming, weather-information services, and information on points of
    interest.

    \item \textbf{Autonomous Driving:}
    Automated and cooperative driving applications rely on timely information
    exchange between vehicles and their surroundings. Representative
    applications include electronic stability control, automatic braking,
    adaptive cruise control, and cooperative awareness. These services impose
    stringent requirements on communication reliability, latency, and
    information freshness.

\end{itemize}

The communication requirements of these application classes vary
considerably. Road-safety and autonomous-driving applications are primarily
latency- and reliability-sensitive, whereas infotainment services are more
throughput-oriented. Consequently, vehicular networks must support
heterogeneous quality-of-service requirements under rapidly changing network
topologies and wireless-channel conditions.

Dedicated Short-Range Communications was among the earliest widely studied
radio technologies for V2X communication. In the United States, DSRC was
developed around operation in the 5.9~GHz band and was supported by the
IEEE 802.11p physical and Medium Access Control (MAC) layers together with
the IEEE 1609 WAVE family of standards
\cite{kenney2011dsrc,morgan2010dsrc}.

IEEE 802.11p evolved from IEEE 802.11a but introduced modifications to enable
communication in rapidly varying vehicular environments
\cite{jiang2008ieee}. Vehicles equipped with On-Board Units (OBUs) can
communicate directly with other vehicles or with RSUs without requiring a
conventional Wi-Fi association procedure.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Safety Message Dissemination}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

A fundamental operation in VANETs is the dissemination of periodic safety
messages. Such messages convey information including vehicle position,
speed, direction, acceleration, and vehicle state. Neighboring vehicles use
these messages to maintain awareness of the surrounding traffic environment.

In DSRC-based networks, safety packets are commonly transmitted through
broadcast communication. This approach minimizes connection-establishment
overhead and allows a single transmission to inform multiple neighboring
vehicles. Vehicle-to-vehicle safety messaging using DSRC has therefore been
an important research topic since the early development of VANETs
\cite{xu2004safety,jiang2006dsrc}.

However, broadcast communication does not inherently guarantee successful
packet reception. As vehicular density increases, a large number of vehicles
may simultaneously compete for channel access. Packet collisions and channel
congestion can consequently reduce the probability that safety information is
successfully delivered.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{MAC Challenges in IEEE 802.11p}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

IEEE 802.11p employs contention-based channel access derived from the IEEE
802.11 family. Although this distributed operation is attractive because it
does not require centralized coordination, its performance can degrade in
dense traffic conditions.

Important MAC-layer challenges include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item contention among a large number of simultaneously transmitting
    vehicles;
    \item hidden-terminal collisions;
    \item rapidly varying numbers of neighboring vehicles;
    \item excessive channel occupancy caused by frequent safety beacons;
    \item lack of deterministic packet-access delay; and
    \item degradation in packet-delivery reliability under high network load.
\end{itemize}

These limitations have motivated numerous adaptive MAC, congestion-control,
and mobility-aware access schemes. For example, mobility-aware MAC protocols
have been developed specifically to reduce packet collisions for vehicular
safety applications \cite{lyu2018momac}, while TDMA-based approaches such as
VeMAC have also been proposed to provide more predictable broadcast access
\cite{omar2013vemac}.

Vehicle-to-Everything communication. Cellular systems provide extensive

infrastructure-based communication through the conventional cellular

\subsection{LTE-V2X Architecture}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

LTE-V2X was standardized by the 3rd Generation Partnership Project (3GPP)
for supporting vehicular communication. Two major communication interfaces
are relevant:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Uu interface:} Conventional communication between a vehicle
    User Equipment (UE) and the cellular base station.

    \item \textbf{PC5 interface:} Direct sidelink communication between
    nearby UEs without routing each packet through the cellular base station.
\end{itemize}

The PC5 sidelink is particularly important for safety applications because
vehicles can directly exchange messages even when conventional cellular
coverage is unavailable.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{LTE-V2X Resource Allocation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

LTE-V2X introduced two principal sidelink resource-allocation mechanisms,
commonly referred to as Mode~3 and Mode~4.

\subsubsection{Mode 3: Network-Scheduled Resource Allocation}

In Mode~3, the serving evolved Node B (eNB) coordinates sidelink radio
resources. The network can therefore use information available at the base
station to reduce conflicting resource assignments.

Two general scheduling strategies can be employed. In dynamic scheduling,
vehicles request radio resources whenever packets need to be transmitted.
Although flexible, frequent requests increase signaling overhead and access
latency.

Semi-Persistent Scheduling (SPS) is more appropriate when messages are
generated periodically. Resources are reserved for several consecutive
transmissions, thereby reducing repeated scheduling requests and control
signaling.

\subsubsection{Mode 4: Autonomous Resource Selection}

Mode~4 allows vehicles to select resources autonomously from a preconfigured
resource pool and can therefore operate outside cellular coverage.

Resource selection employs a sensing-based SPS procedure. Vehicles monitor
sidelink resources and attempt to exclude resources that are likely to be
occupied by neighboring transmitters before selecting candidate transmission
resources. Once selected, the resource can be retained for multiple
transmissions before a resource-reselection procedure is performed.

The autonomous nature of Mode~4 makes it suitable for distributed V2V
communication. However, because vehicles only possess partial information
about the transmission decisions of other UEs, resource collisions remain an
important performance limitation
\cite{gonzalezmartin2018mode4,bazzi2018mode4,molinamasegosa2017mode4}.

\section{5G and NR-V2X Communications}
\label{sec:nrv2x}
% ============================================================

The requirements of advanced automated-driving applications exceed the
capabilities originally considered during the design of LTE-V2X. NR-V2X was
therefore standardized in 3GPP Release~16 to complement LTE-V2X and support
more advanced vehicular services
\cite{3gpp38885,lien2020nr}.

Examples include vehicle platooning, cooperative and extended sensing,
advanced driving, and remote driving. Compared with LTE-V2X, these
applications can involve a combination of periodic and aperiodic traffic,
variable packet sizes, stringent latency constraints, and substantially larger
information payloads.

NR-V2X also expands the supported communication types. Whereas basic
LTE-V2X sidelink operation primarily focuses on broadcast communication,
NR-V2X supports broadcast, groupcast, and unicast communication. Groupcast is
particularly relevant to applications such as platooning, where information is
intended for a specific group of cooperating vehicles.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Flexible NR Numerology}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Another important feature of NR is flexible numerology. LTE-V2X employs a
15~kHz subcarrier spacing, whereas NR-V2X can support larger subcarrier
spacings including 30, 60, and 120~kHz. Increasing the subcarrier spacing
reduces the corresponding slot duration and can therefore help support
latency-sensitive applications.

The appropriate numerology nevertheless depends on factors such as vehicle
speed, propagation conditions, delay requirements, and sensitivity to
inter-carrier and inter-symbol interference.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{NR-V2X Sidelink Resource Allocation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

NR-V2X defines two principal resource-allocation modes.

\subsubsection{Mode 1}

In Mode~1, radio resources are assigned through the cellular network. A gNB
can dynamically schedule sidelink resources or provide configured grants for
repeated transmissions. Network coordination permits the infrastructure to
consider communication requirements such as packet periodicity, packet size,
latency, and reliability when assigning resources.

\subsubsection{Mode 2}

Mode~2 supports autonomous resource selection and can operate without
continuous assistance from the gNB. Vehicles select resources from configured
or preconfigured sidelink resource pools.

Unlike the LTE-V2X Mode~4 design, which was principally developed around
periodic traffic, NR-V2X Mode~2 must support both periodic and aperiodic
traffic. Consequently, enhancements to sensing and resource-selection
procedures are important aspects of NR-V2X resource allocation
\cite{ali2021mode2}.

\section{Mobility Challenges and Safety Message Dissemination}
\label{sec:mobility_challenges}
% ============================================================

Vehicular networking differs fundamentally from many conventional wireless
systems because communication conditions vary rapidly in both space and
time. Several challenges directly influence the reliability of safety-message
dissemination.

\subsection{Rapidly Changing Topology}

Vehicles frequently enter and leave each other's communication range. The
duration of a V2V link may therefore be short, particularly when vehicles
travel in opposite directions or at significantly different speeds.

\subsection{Doppler and Time-Varying Channels}

High vehicle velocities result in significant Doppler shifts and reduced
channel coherence times. Reliable channel estimation and synchronization
become increasingly difficult as relative velocity and carrier frequency
increase.

\subsection{Congestion and Interference}

Dense vehicular scenarios can contain hundreds of simultaneously active
transmitters. In contention-based networks, this produces channel congestion,
whereas sidelink cellular systems must address interference caused by reuse of
the same time-frequency resources.

\subsection{Latency and Reliability}

Safety applications impose substantially more stringent communication
requirements than conventional best-effort wireless services. A packet that
arrives after its safety information has become obsolete can have little
practical value even when it is successfully decoded. Consequently,
vehicular communication protocols must jointly consider packet reception
reliability, latency, packet age, and information-update frequency.

\section{Radio Resource Allocation in Vehicular Networks}
\label{sec:v2x_resource_allocation}
% ============================================================

Radio resource allocation is a fundamental component of vehicular
communication systems because a potentially large number of vehicles must
share a limited set of time--frequency resources while satisfying stringent
requirements on reliability, latency, communication range, and information
freshness. The resource-allocation problem becomes particularly challenging
in vehicular environments because network topology, channel conditions,
vehicle density, and traffic-generation patterns vary rapidly with time.

Broadly, vehicular resource-allocation mechanisms can be classified as
\emph{network-controlled} and \emph{autonomous} schemes. In
network-controlled operation, a cellular base station coordinates sidelink
resources using information available at the infrastructure. In autonomous
operation, each vehicle selects resources from a configured or preconfigured
resource pool based primarily on locally available sensing information.
Hybrid schemes can additionally exploit infrastructure assistance while
retaining distributed resource-selection capability.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Sidelink Resource Pools and Resource Selection}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Direct V2X communication over the PC5 interface is organized using sidelink
resource pools. A resource pool defines the time--frequency resources that
may be used for sidelink transmission. Depending on the operating mode, the
resources within the pool can either be assigned by the cellular
infrastructure or selected autonomously by the transmitting User Equipment
(UE).

The available bandwidth is divided into Physical Resource Blocks (PRBs),
which are grouped into subchannels for sidelink transmission. A Transport
Block (TB), carrying a vehicular packet such as a Cooperative Awareness
Message (CAM) or Basic Safety Message (BSM), is transmitted over the
Physical Sidelink Shared Channel (PSSCH). Associated control information is
conveyed through Sidelink Control Information (SCI), which informs
neighboring vehicles about parameters required for reception and resource
coordination.

The resource-allocation process must therefore determine not only the
time--frequency resources used by each vehicle, but also how long resources
are retained, when they are reselected, and how simultaneously operating
vehicles avoid repeatedly selecting overlapping resources.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Network-Controlled Resource Allocation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Network-controlled resource allocation exploits the global or regional
information available at the cellular infrastructure. In LTE-V2X, this
operation is referred to as Mode~3, whereas NR-V2X introduces the
corresponding Mode~1 operation.

In LTE-V2X Mode~3, an evolved Node B (eNB) manages the sidelink resources
used by vehicles. Resource assignment can be performed through dynamic
scheduling or Semi-Persistent Scheduling (SPS). Under dynamic scheduling,
a vehicle requests resources for each new Transport Block. This provides
flexibility but introduces repeated uplink and downlink control signaling.
SPS instead reserves resources over several transmissions and is therefore
well suited to periodic safety-message generation.

NR-V2X Mode~1 retains network-controlled allocation but introduces
additional scheduling flexibility. A gNB can dynamically assign sidelink
resources through a Dynamic Grant (DG), or pre-allocate resources through a
Configured Grant (CG). Dynamic grants require vehicles to request resources
for individual Transport Blocks, whereas configured grants allow a set of
sidelink resources to be assigned in advance for multiple transmissions.
The latter reduces scheduling latency and signaling overhead for recurring
traffic.

Network-controlled allocation enables coordinated interference management
and potentially efficient spatial reuse. However, it requires network
coverage and introduces control signaling between vehicles and the serving
base station. Its effectiveness can also depend on the availability and
timeliness of Channel State Information (CSI), traffic information, vehicle
position, and interference measurements.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Autonomous Resource Allocation and LTE-V2X Mode~4}
% ------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=10cm, height=6cm]
    {figures/Chapter 9 images/LTEV2X FRAMESTRUCTURE.png}
    \caption{LTE V2X FRAME STRUCTURE}
    \label{fig:v2x_communications}
\end{figure}
Autonomous resource allocation is particularly important for direct
vehicle-to-vehicle communication because safety services should remain
operational even when cellular infrastructure is unavailable. LTE-V2X
Mode~4 therefore allows vehicles to autonomously select sidelink resources
from a configured or preconfigured resource pool.

Mode~4 employs a Sensing-Based Semi-Persistent Scheduling (SB-SPS)
procedure. Rather than selecting a completely new resource for every packet,
a vehicle senses previous sidelink activity, identifies candidate resources,
selects an appropriate resource, and reserves it for several consecutive
transmissions.

The operation of SB-SPS can be summarized through four main components:
the sensing window, selection window, resource exclusion procedure, and
semi-persistent reservation mechanism.

\subsubsection{Sensing Window}

Before performing resource selection, a vehicle observes sidelink activity
during a sensing window. The purpose of sensing is to estimate which
resources have recently been occupied or announced as reserved by
neighboring vehicles.

Sidelink Control Information received from neighboring vehicles indicates
their resource reservations and corresponding Resource Reservation
Intervals (RRIs). The transmitting UE can therefore predict resources that
are likely to be occupied during its future transmissions.

\subsubsection{Selection Window}

When a new resource must be selected, candidate resources are constructed
within a future \emph{selection window}. The beginning of this window
accounts for the UE processing time required to perform resource selection,
while its end must satisfy the latency requirement of the packet awaiting
transmission.

Consequently, the allowable selection window is closely coupled with the
latency requirement of the vehicular application. Safety-critical packets
with short Packet Delay Budgets (PDBs) provide a smaller time interval over
which resources can be selected.

\subsubsection{Resource Exclusion Based on Sensing}

Resources predicted to be occupied by neighboring vehicles are removed
from the candidate set. This prediction is based on decoded SCI messages,
announced reservations, and Reference Signal Received Power (RSRP)
measurements.

A candidate resource associated with another vehicle's reservation is
excluded when the measured RSRP exceeds a configured threshold. If too few
candidate resources remain after exclusion, the RSRP threshold is
progressively relaxed to increase the number of available candidates.

LTE-V2X Mode~4 subsequently evaluates the average Received Signal Strength
Indicator (RSSI) of the surviving candidate resources. Resources
experiencing the lowest average RSSI are preferred, thereby reducing the
likelihood of selecting highly interfered time--frequency resources.

A resource is finally selected from the resulting candidate set. The
distributed nature of this procedure reduces the probability of resource
collisions without requiring centralized coordination.

\subsubsection{Semi-Persistent Reservation}

Once a resource has been selected, SB-SPS allows the vehicle to retain the
same resource for multiple consecutive transmissions. The temporal
separation between successive reserved resources is controlled by the
Resource Reservation Interval (RRI).

A Reselection Counter determines the number of consecutive Transport Blocks
for which the selected resource is maintained. The counter is decremented
after transmission, and when it reaches zero the vehicle decides whether to
retain the existing reservation or perform resource reselection.

If $P_{\mathrm{keep}}$ denotes the probability of retaining the current
resource, the vehicle retains the previously selected resource with
probability $P_{\mathrm{keep}}$ and initiates a new sensing-based selection
procedure with probability
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{reselect}}
    =
    1-P_{\mathrm{keep}}.
\end{equation}

Resource retention reduces reselection overhead and provides temporal
stability. However, excessive retention can preserve resource collisions
when neighboring vehicles have selected overlapping resources. Conversely,
frequent reselection can increase sensing and scheduling overhead.
Consequently, the choice of $P_{\mathrm{keep}}$ creates an important
trade-off between scheduling stability and adaptability.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{NR-V2X Mode~2 Resource Allocation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

NR-V2X Mode~2 generalizes autonomous sidelink resource allocation for the
more heterogeneous traffic patterns and Quality-of-Service (QoS)
requirements associated with advanced vehicular applications. Similar to
LTE-V2X Mode~4, vehicles can autonomously select resources without
continuous gNB assistance. However, the NR-V2X procedure introduces several
important modifications.

First, Mode~2 supports both \emph{dynamic} and \emph{semi-persistent}
resource allocation. Under dynamic allocation, new sidelink resources are
selected for each Transport Block and may additionally be reserved for its
retransmissions. Under semi-persistent allocation, resources are selected
for several consecutive Transport Blocks separated according to an RRI.

NR-V2X also provides substantially greater flexibility in the choice of
RRI. In addition to longer periodic intervals, integer RRI values between
1~ms and 99~ms can be supported, allowing the reservation periodicity to be
better matched to heterogeneous and latency-sensitive traffic patterns.

The selected RRI is also related to the Reselection Counter, which determines
how many consecutive Transport Blocks use the selected resources. After the
counter reaches zero, a vehicle can either retain the selected resources or
perform another resource-selection procedure, thereby maintaining the
semi-persistent character of distributed scheduling.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{NR-V2X Mode~2 Sensing and Candidate Selection}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The NR-V2X Mode~2 resource-selection procedure first constructs a set of
candidate resources within the selection window. Resources that cannot be
reliably evaluated because the UE was transmitting during the corresponding
sensing opportunities can be excluded. Candidate resources predicted to
overlap with reservations announced through first-stage SCI messages from
neighboring UEs are also removed.

The exclusion decision considers the RSRP measured for the received
reservation. A resource is excluded when the measured RSRP exceeds a
priority-dependent threshold. If the percentage of available candidate
resources becomes smaller than a configured value, the RSRP threshold is
increased by 3~dB and the exclusion procedure is repeated until a sufficient
candidate set is available.

Unlike LTE-V2X Mode~4, NR-V2X Mode~2 does not include the additional stage
that ranks surviving candidate resources according to their average RSSI.
After the exclusion procedure, the transmitting UE randomly selects the
required sidelink resources from the available candidate set.

This distinction is important because NR-V2X resource selection should not
be interpreted simply as the LTE Mode~4 SB-SPS procedure operating with a
different numerology.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Resource Reservation, Reselection, and RRI}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Resource reservation is communicated to neighboring vehicles through SCI so
that nearby UEs can avoid resources expected to be occupied in future slots.
For semi-persistent operation, the RRI defines the separation between
resources allocated to consecutive Transport Blocks.

For example, NR-V2X Mode~2 permits a set of configured RRI values including
short intervals between 1~ms and 99~ms together with longer periodic
intervals extending up to 1000~ms. This increased flexibility is important
for supporting both periodic safety messages and emerging applications with
more heterogeneous packet-generation characteristics.

The relationship between RRI and the Reselection Counter controls the
duration of a reservation. Short RRIs generate more frequent transmissions
and therefore require careful congestion management, whereas large RRIs
reduce resource occupancy but may increase the time between consecutive
updates.

Resource reselection may additionally become necessary when the currently
reserved resources cannot accommodate a newly generated Transport Block,
when the transmission cannot satisfy its latency requirement, or when new
sensing information indicates that the selected resource is no longer
suitable.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Re-Evaluation and Pre-Emption in NR-V2X}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

A significant enhancement in NR-V2X Mode~2 is the possibility of
\emph{resource re-evaluation}. After an initial resource has been selected,
the UE can repeat the sensing and exclusion procedure before transmission.
If new information indicates that the selected resource has become
unavailable, another resource can be selected from the updated candidate
set.

This mechanism improves robustness to changes occurring between initial
resource selection and actual transmission, including previously undetected
reservations or rapidly varying interference conditions.

NR-V2X Mode~2 also introduces a \emph{pre-emption} mechanism for handling
traffic with different priorities. When a lower-priority UE determines that
a higher-priority transmission is expected to use one of its reserved
resources, the lower-priority UE can release that reservation and select
another resource. This mechanism enables distributed resource allocation to
better accommodate heterogeneous vehicular services with different latency
and reliability requirements.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Congestion-Aware Resource Allocation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The performance of autonomous sidelink scheduling deteriorates as the number
of simultaneously transmitting vehicles increases. When many vehicles reuse
the same resource pool, the number of collision-free candidate resources
decreases and persistent resource collisions become more likely.

Two commonly used indicators of sidelink congestion are the Channel Busy
Ratio (CBR) and Channel Occupancy Ratio (CR). CBR characterizes the fraction
of observed sidelink resources whose measured received signal strength
exceeds a configured threshold, whereas CR characterizes the fraction of
resources occupied by an individual transmitting UE.

These metrics can be used to adapt transmission and resource-allocation
parameters. Under high channel load, a vehicle may reduce its resource
occupancy by modifying the MCS, transmit power, packet-transmission rate, or
other scheduling parameters. Congestion-aware operation is therefore closely
coupled with both PHY-layer configuration and MAC-layer resource reservation.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Spatial Reuse and Interference-Aware Allocation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Spatial resource reuse is essential for achieving high capacity in dense V2X
networks. The same time--frequency resource can be reused by geographically
separated vehicle pairs when the resulting mutual interference remains
sufficiently small. Resource assignment must therefore account not only for
whether a resource is occupied but also for the spatial relationship among
simultaneous transmitters and receivers.

Location-aware, clustering-based, graph-based, and interference-aware
resource-allocation methods have consequently been investigated for
vehicular systems
\cite{abantoleon2017graph,cecchini2018reuse}. Graph-based formulations, for
example, can represent mutually interfering V2V links as adjacent vertices
or edges and allocate radio resources so that strongly interfering links are
separated whenever possible.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Learning-Based Resource Allocation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The large number of interacting parameters involved in V2X sidelink
operation has motivated learning-based resource-allocation techniques.
Parameters such as the Modulation and Coding Scheme (MCS), transmit power,
subcarrier spacing, Resource Reservation Interval, and resource-retention
probability affect reliability, latency, information freshness, and channel
congestion in a coupled manner.

Reinforcement learning can therefore be used to adapt resource-allocation
decisions according to observed network conditions rather than relying on a
single fixed configuration. The learning state can incorporate information
such as vehicle density, channel occupancy, previous packet-reception
performance, packet size, packet-generation interval, and sensing
measurements, while the action can control one or more PHY- and MAC-layer
parameters.

Such approaches are particularly relevant for NR-V2X Mode~2 because
autonomous vehicles must make distributed scheduling decisions using only
partial observations of the surrounding network. Nevertheless,
learning-based schemes do not replace the underlying NR-V2X resource
selection procedure. Rather, they should operate within the feasible
resource pools, sensing rules, reservation mechanisms, and QoS constraints
defined by the sidelink protocol.

\section{Heterogeneous Vehicular Networks}
\label{sec:heterogeneous_v2x}
% ============================================================

IEEE 802.11-based V2X and cellular V2X possess complementary characteristics.
DSRC provides distributed short-range communication without requiring
cellular infrastructure, whereas cellular technologies provide extensive
network coverage and coordinated resource management.

This observation motivated heterogeneous architectures in which multiple
radio access technologies coexist. Such systems can dynamically select
between IEEE 802.11p and cellular communication according to application
requirements, channel availability, network load, or vehicle location
\cite{abboud2016interworking,ucar2016hybrid}.

However, heterogeneous V2X introduces additional challenges, including
network selection, vertical handover, mobility management, duplicated
signaling, and coordination among different radio interfaces.

% ============================================================
\section{Evolution Toward the Internet of Vehicles}
\label{sec:iov}
% ============================================================

The Internet of Vehicles represents a broader evolution of conventional
vehicular communication. Instead of treating V2X solely as a mechanism for
short-range safety-message exchange, IoV integrates vehicles with road
infrastructure, cellular networks, edge-computing platforms, cloud systems,
and intelligent transportation services.

Future vehicular networks are expected to combine communication with
distributed sensing, computing, artificial intelligence, and automated
driving. Multi-access edge computing can reduce the latency associated with
remote processing, while network slicing and software-defined networking can
provide differentiated services for heterogeneous vehicular applications.

The resulting architecture moves from simple connectivity toward
cooperative intelligence, where vehicles exchange not only status messages
but also sensor observations, environmental information, trajectories, and
control-related information.

\subsection{Traffic-Safety Evaluation of IEEE 802.11p/WAVE Networks}
\label{subsec:wave_safety_evaluation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

An early study investigated the effectiveness of the Wireless
Access in Vehicular Environments (WAVE) protocol in supporting
traffic-safety applications in Vehicular Ad Hoc Networks
(VANETs) \cite{venkatesh2019wave}. The work considered the
IEEE 802.11p physical layer together with the IEEE 1609 WAVE
protocol stack for communication between vehicles and Roadside
Units (RSUs).

In the considered architecture, vehicles are equipped with
On-Board Units (OBUs) and periodically broadcast beacon
messages containing information such as their position and
intended route. RSUs collect information from vehicles within
their communication range and estimate traffic congestion along
the corresponding road segments. When congestion is detected,
the RSU broadcasts the relevant information to approaching
vehicles, enabling them to select an alternative route.

The study was aimed at understanding how communication-network
parameters affect the ultimate traffic-safety performance of a
vehicular network. In particular, the effects of transmission
bit rate, beacon transmission interval, transmit power, vehicle
density, and vehicle mobility were investigated. Unlike studies
that characterize a VANET exclusively through communication
metrics such as throughput, packet loss, or packet delay, the
work considered the vehicle collision rate as an application-level
measure of traffic safety.

A coupled traffic and communication simulation framework was
used for the evaluation. The Simulation of Urban Mobility
(SUMO) was employed to generate vehicular mobility and road
traffic, while OMNeT++ together with the Veins framework was
used to simulate IEEE 802.11p/WAVE communication. Vehicles
periodically transmit their location information, and RSUs
maintain information about traffic conditions in their coverage
regions. When congestion is identified, vehicles can determine
an alternate path using shortest-path routing.

transmissions can increase MAC-layer contention and packet
collisions. Similarly, increasing transmission power initially
improves communication coverage, but excessive transmit power
increases the number of simultaneously contending vehicles and
can consequently increase MAC-layer collisions.

Vehicle density and mobility were also shown to significantly
affect safety performance. Higher vehicular density increases
both road congestion and contention for the shared wireless
channel. The study therefore highlighted that the communication
parameters of a WAVE-based VANET cannot be configured
independently of the underlying traffic conditions.

resource-allocation parameters. Increasing the distance between
communicating vehicles degrades packet-reception performance,
while vehicle density influences the interference and contention
experienced within the shared sidelink resource pool. Similarly,
the MCS and transmit power determine a trade-off between
communication robustness, spectral efficiency, and interference
to neighboring vehicles.

An important feature of NR-V2X is its flexible numerology.
Unlike LTE-V2X, which employs a fixed $15$~kHz subcarrier
spacing, NR-V2X supports multiple subcarrier spacings. Larger
SCS values shorten the corresponding slot duration and improve
robustness against frequency-domain impairments associated with
high mobility. However, a single SCS configuration is not
necessarily suitable for all vehicular conditions.

mechanism was proposed in which the SCS is dynamically
selected according to the operating conditions of the vehicular
network. Rather than retaining a fixed numerology configuration,
the proposed approach adapts the radio-resource configuration
with the objective of improving the Packet Reception Ratio.
Simulation results demonstrated that the adaptive strategy can
provide improved reception performance compared with the
default resource-allocation configuration.

This study therefore extended the vehicular networking research
from the evaluation of contention-based IEEE 802.11p/WAVE
systems toward adaptive radio-resource management in cellular
V2X. It also highlighted the importance of jointly considering
communication reliability and information freshness when
evaluating resource-allocation strategies for highly dynamic
vehicular networks.

\subsection{Learning-Based Resource Allocation for Aperiodic NR-V2X Traffic}
\label{subsec:drl_nrv2x_resource_allocation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The increasing diversity of V2X applications introduces traffic
patterns that differ considerably from the periodic safety-message
generation originally considered in conventional sidelink scheduling.
In particular, NR-V2X must accommodate aperiodic packet generation
and variable packet sizes while maintaining high reliability and
information freshness. To address these requirements, a learning-based
adaptive resource-allocation framework was developed for NR-V2X
Mode~2 \cite{indulekha2024drl}.

NR-V2X Mode~2 enables vehicles to autonomously select sidelink
resources using Sensing-Based Semi-Persistent Scheduling (SB-SPS).
Under SB-SPS, a vehicle reserves resources for a number of
consecutive transmissions according to a Resource Reservation
Interval (RRI). When the reselection counter expires, the vehicle
either retains the previously selected resource with probability
$P_{\mathrm{keep}}$ or performs a new resource-selection procedure.
Candidate resources are identified through sidelink sensing using
Sidelink Control Information (SCI), Reference Signal Received Power
(RSRP), and Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI).

Although SB-SPS is effective for periodic message generation, its
semi-persistent reservation mechanism can lead to inefficient resource
utilization when traffic becomes aperiodic. If the next packet is
generated later than the previously reserved transmission opportunity,
the reserved resource may remain unused. Similarly, variations in
packet size can cause a previously reserved resource to become
insufficient for a subsequent larger packet, forcing the vehicle to
perform resource reselection.

\begin{figure}[!ht]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=10cm, height=6cm]
    {figures/Chapter 9 images/Aperiodic.png}
    \caption{Resource underutilization scenarios}
    \label{fig:v2x_communications}
\end{figure}

To improve the adaptability of sidelink resource reservation, three
important parameters were jointly considered: the Modulation and
Coding Scheme (MCS), the Resource Reservation Interval (RRI), and
the resource Keep-Alive probability $P_{\mathrm{keep}}$. The MCS
determines the trade-off between transmission rate and link
robustness, while the RRI controls the temporal spacing between
reserved transmission opportunities. The parameter
$P_{\mathrm{keep}}$ determines whether previously selected sidelink
resources are retained after expiration of the reselection counter.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{Double Deep Q-Learning for Adaptive Resource Allocation in NR-V2X}
\label{alg:ddqn_nrv2x}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\State Initialize Q-network with random weights $\theta$
\State Initialize target Q-network with weights $\theta^{-} \gets \theta$
\State Initialize replay memory $\mathcal{D}$

\For{episode $=1$ to $M$}

    \State Initialize state $s_0$ based on V2X parameters

    \For{$t=1$ to $T$}

        \State With probability $\epsilon$, select a random action
        \[
        a_t = (\mathrm{MCS},\mathrm{RRI},P_{\mathrm{keep}})
        \]

        \State Otherwise, select
        \[
        a_t =
        \arg\max_{a}
        Q(s_t,a;\theta)
        \]

        \State Execute action $a_t$ in the V2X environment
        \State Observe reward $r_t$ and next state $s_{t+1}$

        \State Store transition
        \[
        (s_t,a_t,r_t,s_{t+1})
        \]
        in replay memory $\mathcal{D}$

        \State Sample a minibatch of transitions
        \[
        (s_j,a_j,r_j,s_{j+1})
        \]
        from $\mathcal{D}$

        \State Select the next action using the online Q-network:
        \[
        a_j^{*}
        =
        \arg\max_{a}
        Q(s_{j+1},a;\theta)
        \]

        \If{$s_{j+1}$ is terminal}
            \State $y_j \gets r_j$
        \Else
            \State
            \[
            y_j
            \gets
            r_j
            +
            \gamma
            Q(s_{j+1},a_j^{*};\theta^{-})
            \]
        \EndIf

        \State Update $\theta$ by minimizing
        \[
        \left(
        y_j-Q(s_j,a_j;\theta)
        \right)^2
        \]

        \If{$C$ steps have passed}
            \State Update target network:
            \[
            \theta^{-} \gets \theta
            \]
        \EndIf

        \State $s_t \gets s_{t+1}$

    \EndFor

\EndFor

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

A Double Deep Q-Network (DDQN)-based resource-allocation
mechanism was developed to dynamically adapt these parameters
according to the observed vehicular-network conditions. The
optimization objective balances communication reliability,
information freshness, and channel congestion through a utility of
the form
\begin{equation}
    \Phi =
    \gamma_{1}\,\mathrm{PRR}
    -
    \gamma_{2}\,\mathrm{DA}
    -
    \gamma_{3}\,\mathrm{CBR},
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{PRR}$ denotes the Packet Reception Ratio,
$\mathrm{DA}$ denotes the Data Age, $\mathrm{CBR}$ denotes the
Channel Busy Ratio, and $\gamma_{1}$, $\gamma_{2}$, and
$\gamma_{3}$ determine their relative importance.

The corresponding reinforcement-learning problem is represented as
a Markov Decision Process. The state contains information describing
the current channel occupancy, packet-transmission status, timing
information, and vehicle-position updates. The action taken by the
learning agent consists of selecting the triplet
\begin{equation}
    a_t =
    \left[
    \mathrm{MCS}(t),
    \mathrm{RRI}(t),
    P_{\mathrm{keep}}(t)
    \right].
\end{equation}
The reward is derived from the reliability--freshness--congestion
utility $\Phi$, enabling the agent to learn resource configurations
that respond to changes in packet generation, packet size, and
network conditions.

previously observed state--action--reward transitions.

NR-V2X Mode~2 operation. The experiments considered variations in
vehicle density, packet size, packet-generation interval, MCS,
RRI, and resource keep probability. The results showed that
dynamically adapting these parameters provides a better balance
between packet-reception reliability and information freshness than
using fixed parameter configurations.

RRI adaptation was particularly beneficial for aperiodic traffic,
where a fixed reservation interval can result in either unused
resources or delayed information updates.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Dynamic Resource and Power Allocation for ISAC-Aided NR-V2X}
\label{subsec:isac_nrv2x_resource_power}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

As vehicular communication systems evolve toward increasingly automated
operation, communication reliability alone is insufficient to characterize
the performance of a V2X network. Advanced vehicular applications require
vehicles to exchange information while simultaneously sensing neighboring
vehicles and the surrounding environment. This motivates the use of
Integrated Sensing and Communication (ISAC), where a common waveform,
spectrum, and radio-frequency front end are exploited for both data
transmission and environmental sensing.

A dynamic resource- and power-allocation framework was therefore developed
for an ISAC-aided NR-V2X network, in which the Modulation and Coding Scheme
(MCS), Subcarrier Spacing (SCS), and transmission power are jointly adapted
using Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL)
\cite{indulekha2024isac}. The objective is to improve communication
reliability while simultaneously maintaining low channel congestion and
accurate vehicular sensing.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{ISAC-Aided Vehicular System Model}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Consider a vehicular network consisting of $N_v$ vehicles represented by

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{V}
=
\left\{
v_1,v_2,\ldots,v_{N_v}
\right\}.
\label{eq:isac_vehicle_set}
\end{equation}

Each vehicle is equipped with an in-band full-duplex transceiver and
periodically transmits Cooperative Awareness Messages (CAMs). At the same
time, the backscattered communication waveform is processed for radar
sensing. Hence, a transmitting vehicle can communicate with neighboring
vehicles while estimating their range and relative velocity from the
reflected waveform.

Let the vehicular density be denoted by $\rho$, representing the number of
vehicles per unit road length or spatial region considered in the
simulation. The density directly affects both communication interference and
radar interference and therefore influences the communication and sensing
performance simultaneously.

    \centering

    {figures/Chapter 9 images/ISACPAPER.png}
    \caption{DDQN-based dynamic resource- and power-allocation architecture
    for the ISAC-aided NR-V2X network.}
    \label{fig:isac_v2x_ddqn}
\end{figure}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{OFDM-Based ISAC Transmit Signal}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The communication waveform is an OFDM signal. For the $m$th OFDM symbol,
the transmitted baseband waveform is represented as

\begin{equation}
s_m(t)
=
\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}
\sqrt{P_{n,m}}\,
x_{n,m}
e^{j2\pi n\Delta f t}
\operatorname{rect}
\left(
\frac{t-mT_{\mathrm{sym}}}
{T_{\mathrm{sym}}}
\right),
\label{eq:isac_ofdm_tx}
\end{equation}

where $N$ denotes the number of subcarriers, $\Delta f$ is the
subcarrier spacing, $T_{\mathrm{sym}}$ is the OFDM symbol duration,
$P_{n,m}$ denotes the transmit power assigned to the $n$th subcarrier
of the $m$th OFDM symbol, and $x_{n,m}$ represents the corresponding
modulated data symbol.

For $M$ OFDM symbols and $N$ subcarriers, the transmitted symbols can be
written in matrix form as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{X}
=
\left[
x_{n,m}
\right]
\in
\mathbb{C}^{N\times M}.
\label{eq:isac_symbol_matrix}
\end{equation}

This signal performs a dual function: it carries vehicular communication
data over the sidelink and acts as the probing waveform for sensing nearby
vehicles.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Radar Echo and Target Model}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Consider a neighboring vehicle modeled as a point target located at distance
$d$ from the sensing vehicle and moving with relative velocity $v$. The
propagation delay associated with the round-trip radar path is

\begin{equation}
\tau
=
\frac{2d}{c},
\label{eq:isac_delay}
\end{equation}

where $c$ denotes the speed of light.

The corresponding Doppler frequency is

\begin{equation}
f_D
=
\frac{2f_c v}{c},
\label{eq:isac_doppler}
\end{equation}

where $f_c$ is the carrier frequency.

The complex-baseband reflected signal can therefore be expressed as

\begin{equation}
r(t)
=
\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}
h\,
s_m(t-\tau)
e^{j2\pi f_D mT_{\mathrm{sym}}}
+
n(t),
\label{eq:isac_echo_signal}
\end{equation}

where $h$ denotes the complex target channel coefficient and $n(t)$
represents Additive White Gaussian Noise (AWGN).

The target-dependent channel coefficient can be written as

\begin{equation}
h
=
|h|e^{j\angle h},
\label{eq:isac_channel_coeff}
\end{equation}

with magnitude related to the ratio between received and transmitted power,

\begin{equation}
|h|
=
\sqrt{\frac{P_R}{P_T}}.
\label{eq:isac_channel_gain}
\end{equation}

Using the monostatic radar equation, the received echo power is modeled as

\begin{equation}
P_R
=
P_T
\frac{
G^2
\lambda^2
\sigma
}{
(4\pi)^3d^4
},
\label{eq:isac_radar_equation}
\end{equation}

where $P_T$ denotes the transmitted power, $G$ denotes the antenna gain,
$\lambda$ is the wavelength, and $\sigma$ denotes the radar cross section
of the reflecting vehicle.

Equation~\eqref{eq:isac_radar_equation} explicitly demonstrates the
strong dependence of sensing performance on target range, since the echo
power decreases approximately as $d^{-4}$.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Frequency-Domain Radar Observation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

After cyclic-prefix removal and FFT processing, the received sample
corresponding to the $n$th subcarrier and $m$th OFDM symbol can be written as

\begin{equation}
y_{n,m}
=
\sqrt{P_{n,m}}\,
x_{n,m}\,
T_{\mathrm{sym}}\,
h\,
e^{-j2\pi n\Delta f\tau}
e^{j2\pi f_DmT_{\mathrm{sym}}}
+
z_{n,m},
\label{eq:isac_fft_observation}
\end{equation}

where $z_{n,m}$ denotes the frequency-domain noise sample.

The complete received-symbol matrix is

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y}
=
\left[
y_{n,m}
\right]
\in
\mathbb{C}^{N\times M}.
\label{eq:isac_received_matrix}
\end{equation}

Since the transmitted symbols are known at the sensing transmitter, their
modulation can be removed by element-wise division, producing an estimate of
the sensing channel-response matrix,

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{H}
=
\mathbf{Y}
\oslash
\mathbf{X},
\label{eq:isac_channel_response}
\end{equation}

where $\oslash$ denotes element-wise division.

The delay--Doppler response is subsequently obtained through a
two-dimensional periodogram,

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{P}(n,m)
=
\left|
\sum_{\ell=0}^{N-1}
\sum_{q=0}^{M-1}
H_{\ell,q}
e^{-j2\pi qm/M}
e^{j2\pi \ell n/N}
\right|^2.
\label{eq:isac_periodogram}
\end{equation}

The resulting two-dimensional representation forms a delay--Doppler map,
whose local maxima correspond to potential radar targets.

A Cell-Averaging Constant False Alarm Rate (CA-CFAR) detector is employed
to identify target peaks using an adaptive detection threshold. Let
$(\hat{\tau},\hat{f}_D)$ denote the delay and Doppler estimates obtained
from the detected peak. The corresponding estimated range is

\begin{equation}
\hat{d}
=
\frac{c\hat{\tau}}{2},
\label{eq:isac_range_estimate}
\end{equation}

and the estimated relative velocity is

\begin{equation}
\hat{v}
=
\frac{c\hat{f}_D}{2f_c}.
\label{eq:isac_velocity_estimate}
\end{equation}

Therefore, the communication waveform provides not only V2X information
exchange but also range and relative-speed information about surrounding
vehicles.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Sensing Accuracy: CRLB for Range and Velocity}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The sensing performance is characterized through the
Cram\'er--Rao Lower Bound (CRLB), which specifies the minimum achievable
variance of an unbiased estimator under the assumed observation model.

For range estimation, the CRLB considered in the framework is

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CRLB}_{d}
=
\frac{
3c^2(P_n+I)
}{
8\pi^2
P_R
MN
\left(N^2-1\right)
\Delta f^2
},
\label{eq:isac_crlb_range}
\end{equation}

where $P_n$ denotes the noise power, $I$ denotes the aggregate interference,
and $P_R$ denotes the radar echo power.

Similarly, the CRLB for relative-speed estimation is

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CRLB}_{v}
=
\frac{
3c^2(P_n+I)
}{
8\pi^2
P_R
f_c^2
MN
\left(M^2-1\right)
T_{\mathrm{sym}}^2
}.
\label{eq:isac_crlb_velocity}
\end{equation}

These expressions reveal the principal coupling between the physical-layer
resource parameters and sensing accuracy. Increasing $P_T$ increases the
received echo power $P_R$, which can reduce both sensing CRLBs. Similarly,
the SCS $\Delta f$ directly affects the range-estimation bound, while the
number of OFDM symbols and the OFDM symbol duration influence Doppler and
velocity estimation.

For reporting sensing accuracy, the corresponding root-CRLB quantities can
be written as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RCRLB}_{d}
=
\sqrt{\mathrm{CRLB}_{d}},
\label{eq:isac_root_crlb_range}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RCRLB}_{v}
=
\sqrt{\mathrm{CRLB}_{v}}.
\label{eq:isac_root_crlb_velocity}
\end{equation}

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{NR-V2X Sidelink Resource Allocation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The communication component operates over the NR-V2X sidelink. Two
resource-allocation modes are considered. In Mode~1, the cellular
infrastructure assigns sidelink transmission resources, whereas in Mode~2
vehicles autonomously select resources from the sidelink resource pool.

The considered autonomous allocation follows the sensing-based
semi-persistent resource-allocation procedure used in the underlying
simulation framework. A transmitting vehicle reserves resources for several
consecutive Transport Blocks (TBs), with the reservation periodicity
controlled by the Resource Reservation Interval (RRI).

Let $R_i(t)$ denote the sidelink resource selected by vehicle $v_i$ at
time $t$. The corresponding reservation interval determines the future
resource occupation according to

\begin{equation}
t_{i,\ell}
=
t_i^{(0)}
+
\ell T_{\mathrm{RRI}},
\qquad
\ell
=
0,1,\ldots,C_i-1,
\label{eq:isac_rri_reservation}
\end{equation}

where $C_i$ denotes the Reselection Counter associated with vehicle $v_i$.

The counter evolves after each Transport Block transmission as

\begin{equation}
C_i(t+1)
=
C_i(t)-1,
\label{eq:isac_rc_update}
\end{equation}

until it reaches zero.

At reselection, the vehicle either retains or changes the current resource.
If $P_{\mathrm{keep}}$ denotes the resource-retention probability, then

\begin{equation}
\Pr
\left[
R_i(t+1)=R_i(t)
\right]
=
P_{\mathrm{keep}},
\label{eq:isac_keep_probability}
\end{equation}

while

\begin{equation}
\Pr
\left[
R_i(t+1)\neq R_i(t)
\right]
=
1-P_{\mathrm{keep}}.
\label{eq:isac_reselection_probability}
\end{equation}

During resource reselection, candidate sidelink resources are identified
within a selection window. Resources announced by neighboring vehicles
through Sidelink Control Information (SCI) are removed when their measured
Reference Signal Received Power (RSRP) exceeds the sensing threshold. The
remaining candidate resources constitute an available-resource set

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_1
=
\left\{
r\in\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SW}}
:
\mathrm{RSRP}_{r}
<
\Gamma_{\mathrm{RSRP}}
\right\},
\label{eq:isac_candidate_set_l1}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{SW}}$ denotes the resources in the selection
window and $\Gamma_{\mathrm{RSRP}}$ denotes the RSRP exclusion threshold.

If the candidate set is too small, the RSRP threshold is increased and the
resource-exclusion operation is repeated.

A lower-interference subset can subsequently be represented as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_2
\subseteq
\mathcal{L}_1,
\label{eq:isac_candidate_set_l2}
\end{equation}

where the resources in $\mathcal{L}_2$ correspond to candidate resources
with lower measured Received Signal Strength Indicator (RSSI). The final
resource is selected from the candidate set according to the decentralized
selection mechanism.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Communication Performance Metrics}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Communication reliability is characterized using the Packet Reception
Ratio (PRR). For vehicle $v_i$, the PRR over an observation interval of
$T$ packet-generation instances is defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PRR}_{v_i}
=
\frac{1}{T}
\sum_{k=1}^{T}
\frac{
P_{\mathrm{received}}^{(i,k)}
}{
P_{\mathrm{total}}^{(i,k)}
},
\label{eq:isac_prr}
\end{equation}

where $P_{\mathrm{received}}^{(i,k)}$ denotes the number of successfully
received packets associated with the $k$th observation and
$P_{\mathrm{total}}^{(i,k)}$ denotes the number of intended neighboring
receivers.

The Channel Busy Ratio (CBR) is used as an indicator of sidelink
congestion and is defined as

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CBR}
=
\frac{
T_{\mathrm{busy}}
}{
T_{\mathrm{total}}
},
\label{eq:isac_cbr}
\end{equation}

where $T_{\mathrm{busy}}$ denotes the duration for which the sidelink
channel is sensed as occupied and $T_{\mathrm{total}}$ denotes the total
observation interval.

The communication objective therefore seeks a high PRR while simultaneously
avoiding excessive CBR.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Joint Communication--Sensing Utility}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The communication and sensing objectives are inherently coupled through
the resource-allocation parameters. The MCS influences spectral efficiency
and packet-decoding robustness, SCS affects the OFDM time--frequency
structure and the sensing resolution, and transmission power influences
both communication coverage and radar echo strength.

To capture these conflicting objectives, the joint utility is defined as

\begin{equation}
\Psi
=
\delta_1
\mathrm{PRR}
-
\delta_2
\mathrm{CBR}
-
\delta_3
\left(
\mathrm{CRLB}_{d}
+
\mathrm{CRLB}_{v}
\right),
\label{eq:isac_joint_utility}
\end{equation}

where $\delta_1$, $\delta_2$, and $\delta_3$ are weighting coefficients
that determine the relative importance of communication reliability,
channel congestion, and sensing accuracy.

The corresponding resource- and power-allocation problem is formulated as

\begin{equation}
\underset{
M_k,\Delta f,P_T
}{
\operatorname{maximize}}
\quad
\Psi,
\label{eq:isac_optimization}
\end{equation}

subject to

\begin{align}
M_{k,\min}
&\leq
M_k
\leq
M_{k,\max},
\label{eq:isac_constraint_mcs}
\\
\Delta f_{\min}
&\leq
\Delta f
\leq
\Delta f_{\max},
\label{eq:isac_constraint_scs}
\\
P_{T,\min}
&\leq
P_T
\leq
P_{T,\max}.
\label{eq:isac_constraint_power}
\end{align}

The feasible parameter ranges are constrained by the sidelink
configuration adopted in the system.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Markov Decision Process Formulation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Because vehicular density, packet traffic, interference, and sensing
conditions vary dynamically, the resource-allocation problem is modeled as
a Markov Decision Process (MDP).

The state at time $t$ is represented as

\begin{equation}
s_t
=
\left\{
\mathcal{P}(t),
\mathcal{C}(t),
\mathcal{S}(t),
\mathcal{T}(t)
\right\},
\label{eq:isac_mdp_state}
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{P}(t)$ denotes packet-transmission information,
$\mathcal{C}(t)$ denotes congestion information,
$\mathcal{S}(t)$ denotes sensing-quality information, and
$\mathcal{T}(t)$ contains timing-related information.

The packet-related component can include quantities such as packet size and
PRR, while the congestion component captures the CBR. The sensing component
contains the range and speed CRLBs.

The action selected by the learning agent is

\begin{equation}
a_t
=
\left[
M_k(t),
\Delta f(t),
P_T(t)
\right].
\label{eq:isac_mdp_action}
\end{equation}

The considered discrete action dimensions are

\begin{equation}
M_k(t)
\in
\left\{
1,2,\ldots,28
\right\},
\label{eq:isac_action_mcs}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\Delta f(t)
\in
\left\{
15,30,60
\right\}
\ \mathrm{kHz},
\label{eq:isac_action_scs}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
P_T(t)
\in
\left\{
10,11,\ldots,23
\right\}
\ \mathrm{dBm}.
\label{eq:isac_action_power}
\end{equation}

The reward is directly derived from the joint communication--sensing
utility,

\begin{equation}
r_t
=
\Psi_t.
\label{eq:isac_mdp_reward}
\end{equation}

Thus, the agent receives a larger reward when PRR increases and a smaller
reward when CBR or the sensing CRLBs increase.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{DDQN-Based Resource and Power Adaptation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

A Double Deep Q-Network (DDQN) is employed to learn the resource-control
policy. Let $Q(s,a;\boldsymbol{\theta})$ denote the primary Q-network with
parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$, and let
$Q'(s,a;\boldsymbol{\theta}')$ denote the target network.

An $\epsilon$-greedy policy is employed during training,

\begin{equation}
a_t
=
\begin{cases}
\text{random action},
&
\text{with probability } \epsilon,
\\[1mm]
\displaystyle
\arg\max_{a}
Q
\left(
s_t,a;\boldsymbol{\theta}
\right),
&
\text{with probability }1-\epsilon.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:isac_epsilon_greedy}
\end{equation}

After executing action $a_t$, the agent observes the reward $r_t$ and
next state $s_{t+1}$. The resulting experience

\begin{equation}
e_t
=
\left(
s_t,a_t,r_t,s_{t+1}
\right)
\label{eq:isac_experience_tuple}
\end{equation}

is stored in an experience-replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$.

For a sampled transition
$(s_j,a_j,r_j,s_{j+1})$, the target value is computed as

\begin{equation}
y_j
=
\begin{cases}
r_j,
&
s_{j+1}
\text{ terminal},
\\[1mm]
r_j
+
\gamma
\displaystyle
\max_{a'}
Q'
\left(
s_{j+1},a';\boldsymbol{\theta}'
\right),
&
\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:isac_ddqn_target}
\end{equation}

where $\gamma$ denotes the discount factor.

The primary Q-network is trained by minimizing the temporal-difference
loss

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\theta}
\right)
=
\left[
y_j
-
Q
\left(
s_j,a_j;\boldsymbol{\theta}
\right)
\right]^2.
\label{eq:isac_ddqn_loss}
\end{equation}

More generally, over a mini-batch $\mathcal{B}$, the loss can be written as

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\theta}
\right)
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}
\sum_{j\in\mathcal{B}}
\left[
y_j
-
Q
\left(
s_j,a_j;\boldsymbol{\theta}
\right)
\right]^2.
\label{eq:isac_ddqn_batch_loss}
\end{equation}

The network parameters are updated through gradient descent,

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}
\leftarrow
\boldsymbol{\theta}
-
\alpha
\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}
\mathcal{L}
\left(
\boldsymbol{\theta}
\right),
\label{eq:isac_gradient_update}
\end{equation}

where $\alpha$ is the learning rate.

The target network is updated using a soft parameter update,

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\theta}'
\leftarrow
\tau_{\mathrm{DDQN}}
\boldsymbol{\theta}
+
\left(
1-\tau_{\mathrm{DDQN}}
\right)
\boldsymbol{\theta}',
\label{eq:isac_soft_update}
\end{equation}

where $\tau_{\mathrm{DDQN}}$ denotes the target-network update coefficient.

The exploration probability is gradually reduced during training,

\begin{equation}
\epsilon_{t+1}
=
\max
\left(
\epsilon_{\min},
\kappa_{\epsilon}\epsilon_t
\right),
\label{eq:isac_epsilon_decay}
\end{equation}

where $\kappa_{\epsilon}<1$ is the exploration-decay factor.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Coupling Between Communication and Sensing}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The central feature of the proposed framework is the coupling between the
communication and sensing objectives. In particular,

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{PRR}
=
f_{\mathrm{c}}
\left(
M_k,\Delta f,P_T,\rho,\ldots
\right),
\label{eq:isac_prr_dependency}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CBR}
=
f_{\mathrm{b}}
\left(
M_k,\Delta f,P_T,\rho,\ldots
\right),
\label{eq:isac_cbr_dependency}
\end{equation}

while

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CRLB}_{d}
=
f_{\mathrm{r}}
\left(
P_T,\Delta f,
P_n,I,d,N,M
\right),
\label{eq:isac_range_dependency}
\end{equation}

and

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CRLB}_{v}
=
f_{\mathrm{v}}
\left(
P_T,
T_{\mathrm{sym}},
f_c,
P_n,
I,
d,
N,
M
\right).
\label{eq:isac_velocity_dependency}
\end{equation}

Hence, changing a single radio parameter can simultaneously alter
communication reliability, channel occupancy, and sensing accuracy. The
resource-allocation problem therefore cannot generally be decomposed into
independent communication and sensing optimizations.

For example, increasing $P_T$ may improve communication coverage and
increase radar echo power,

\begin{equation}
P_R
\propto
P_T,
\label{eq:isac_power_echo_relation}
\end{equation}

which tends to reduce

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{CRLB}_{d},
\,
\mathrm{CRLB}_{v}
\propto
\frac{1}{P_R}.
\label{eq:isac_crlb_power_relation}
\end{equation}

However, larger transmission power can also increase the interference
experienced by neighboring vehicles and influence the channel occupancy
conditions. Similarly, modifying $\Delta f$ changes the OFDM waveform,
resource structure, and the range-estimation CRLB. The DDQN therefore seeks
a policy that adapts the three control parameters jointly rather than
optimizing them independently.

\subsubsection{Simulation-Based Evaluation}

The framework was evaluated using an extended WiLabV2Xsim simulator
incorporating both NR-V2X communication and sensing functionality. The
simulation considered a road length of $1000$~m, a bandwidth of
$10$~MHz, packet sizes between $100$ and $1000$~bytes, vehicle densities

\subsection{RIS-Assisted Full-Duplex MAC with Predictive QoS for NR-V2X}
\label{subsec:ris_fd_mac_nrv2x}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The subsequent research extended NR-V2X resource management
toward an intelligent MAC-layer framework that jointly incorporates
Reconfigurable Intelligent Surfaces (RISs), full-duplex communication,
machine-learning-based Quality-of-Service (QoS) prediction, and
adaptive sidelink resource-retention decisions
\cite{indulekha2025risfd}.

The framework is designed for NR-V2X Mode~2 operation, in which
vehicles autonomously select sidelink resources from a configured
resource pool. In dense vehicular environments, autonomous sensing
may fail to identify all interfering transmitters, particularly when
vehicles are mutually blocked or hidden from one another. This can
result in persistent resource collisions and reduced Packet Reception
Ratio (PRR). RIS-assisted propagation and predictive MAC decisions
are therefore incorporated to improve the reliability of distributed
sidelink operation.

\subsubsection{RIS-Assisted V2V Channel Model}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Consider a transmitting vehicle $v_i$ communicating with a receiving
vehicle $v_j$. In addition to the direct V2V propagation path, one or
more RIS panels can provide reflected propagation paths around
obstructions.

For the $k$th RIS containing $N_k$ reflecting elements, the corresponding
phase-shift matrix is written as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{\Phi}_{k}
=
\operatorname{diag}
\left(
e^{j\phi_{k,1}},
e^{j\phi_{k,2}},
\ldots,
e^{j\phi_{k,N_k}}
\right),
\label{eq:ris_phase_matrix}
\end{equation}

where $\phi_{k,n}$ denotes the phase shift introduced by the $n$th
element of RIS $k$.

The resulting effective V2V channel between vehicles $i$ and $j$ can
be represented as

\begin{equation}
h_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}
=
h_{ij}^{\mathrm{dir}}
+
\sum_{k=1}^{K}
\mathbf{h}_{R_kj}^{H}
\mathbf{\Phi}_{k}
\mathbf{h}_{iR_k},
\label{eq:ris_effective_channel}
\end{equation}

where $h_{ij}^{\mathrm{dir}}$ denotes the direct V2V channel,
$\mathbf{h}_{iR_k}$ denotes the channel between vehicle $i$ and RIS
$k$, and $\mathbf{h}_{R_kj}$ represents the channel between RIS $k$
and receiving vehicle $j$.

The received signal can consequently be expressed as

\begin{equation}
y_j
=
\sqrt{P_i}\,
h_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}x_i
+
\sum_{\ell\neq i}
\sqrt{P_{\ell}}\,
h_{\ell j}^{\mathrm{eff}}x_{\ell}
+
n_j,
\label{eq:ris_received_signal}
\end{equation}

where $P_i$ is the transmission power of vehicle $i$, $x_i$ is the
transmitted symbol, the summation represents co-channel sidelink
interference, and $n_j$ denotes receiver noise.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Full-Duplex Operation and Residual Self-Interference}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Vehicles are assumed to employ full-duplex transceivers so that
transmission and reception can occur simultaneously over the sidelink.
Although self-interference cancellation is applied, a residual
self-interference component remains.

Let $\beta_{\mathrm{SI}}\in[0,1]$ denote the fraction of residual
self-interference after cancellation. The received SINR at vehicle
$j$ can then be expressed as

\begin{equation}
\Gamma_{ij}
=
\frac{
P_i
\left|
h_{ij}^{\mathrm{eff}}
\right|^2
}{
\displaystyle
\sum_{\ell\neq i}
P_{\ell}
\left|
h_{\ell j}^{\mathrm{eff}}
\right|^2
+
\beta_{\mathrm{SI}}P_j
+
\sigma_j^2
},
\label{eq:ris_fd_sinr}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma_j^2$ denotes receiver noise power.

The residual self-interference term introduces an additional MAC-layer
trade-off. Full-duplex operation improves simultaneous information
exchange and resource awareness, but excessive residual interference
can reduce decoding reliability. This information is therefore also
considered when deciding whether RIS assistance should be activated.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Predictive QoS Estimation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

Conventional autonomous resource selection reacts primarily to currently
available sensing information. The proposed framework instead introduces
a predictive layer that estimates the future communication quality before
the resource-retention decision is made.

Let the input feature vector at decision instant $t$ be

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{x}(t)
=
\left[
\rho(t),
d_{ij}(t),
P_T(t),
\mathrm{MCS}(t),
\mathrm{CBR}(t),
\mathrm{PRR}(t),
\Gamma_{ij}(t),
\ldots
\right]^{T},
\label{eq:ris_ml_features}
\end{equation}

where $\rho(t)$ denotes vehicle density and $d_{ij}(t)$ denotes the
inter-vehicle distance.

A supervised-learning model $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ML}}(\cdot)$ maps the
observed system parameters to predicted QoS metrics,

\begin{equation}
\left[
\widehat{\mathrm{PRR}}(t+1),
\widehat{\mathrm{CBR}}(t+1)
\right]
=
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{ML}}
\left(
\mathbf{x}(t)
\right).
\label{eq:ris_qos_prediction}
\end{equation}

Four ensemble regressors were considered for this prediction task:
Random Forest, eXtreme Gradient Boosting, CatBoost, and Gradient
Boosting Regression. Among the evaluated alternatives, Random Forest
was selected for integration into the MAC framework based on its
prediction performance.

\subsubsection{QoS-Aware RIS Activation}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

RIS assistance is invoked only when the predicted operating conditions
indicate that conventional direct V2V communication may be insufficient.
A binary RIS-control variable is defined as

\begin{equation}
u_{\mathrm{RIS}}(t)
\in
\{0,1\},
\label{eq:ris_activation_variable}
\end{equation}

where $u_{\mathrm{RIS}}(t)=1$ denotes RIS-assisted operation.

A representative activation rule can be written as

\begin{equation}
u_{\mathrm{RIS}}(t)
=
\begin{cases}
1,
&
\widehat{\mathrm{PRR}}(t+1)
<
\Gamma_{\mathrm{PRR}},
\\
1,
&
\widehat{\mathrm{CBR}}(t+1)
>
\Gamma_{\mathrm{CBR}},
\\
1,
&
I_{\mathrm{SI}}(t)
>
\Gamma_{\mathrm{SI}},
\\
0,
&
\text{otherwise},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:ris_activation_rule}
\end{equation}

where $\Gamma_{\mathrm{PRR}}$, $\Gamma_{\mathrm{CBR}}$, and
$\Gamma_{\mathrm{SI}}$ denote reliability, congestion, and residual
self-interference thresholds, respectively.

Thus, RIS resources are not continuously activated; instead, they are
invoked selectively when the predicted link or congestion state indicates
a potential degradation in sidelink performance.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Adaptive Beacon-Resource Retention}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The predictive QoS information is also used to modify the conventional
semi-persistent resource-retention decision.

Let $B_i(t)$ denote the Beacon Resource (BR) reserved by vehicle $i$.
Under satisfactory predicted link conditions, the conventional resource
keep probability $P_{\mathrm{keep}}$ is applied,

\begin{equation}
\Pr
\left[
B_i(t+1)=B_i(t)
\right]
=
P_{\mathrm{keep}}.
\label{eq:ris_standard_keep}
\end{equation}

When

\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{PRR}}(t+1)
<
\Gamma_{\mathrm{PRR}},
\label{eq:ris_low_prr_condition}
\end{equation}

a Markovian reliability assessment is performed before retaining the
current BR.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{DTMC-Based Long-Term Reliability Assessment}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The BR reliability is modeled through a two-state Discrete-Time Markov
Chain (DTMC),

\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}
=
\left\{
S,F
\right\},
\label{eq:ris_dtmc_states}
\end{equation}

where $S$ represents successful packet reception and $F$ represents
unsuccessful reception.

The transition matrix is written as

\begin{equation}
\mathbf{P}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
p_{SS} & p_{SF}
\\
p_{FS} & p_{FF}
\end{bmatrix},
\label{eq:ris_dtmc_matrix}
\end{equation}

with

\begin{equation}
p_{SS}+p_{SF}=1,
\qquad
p_{FS}+p_{FF}=1.
\label{eq:ris_dtmc_row_sum}
\end{equation}

Let

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\pi}
=
\left[
\pi_S,
\pi_F
\right]
\end{equation}

denote the stationary distribution. It satisfies

\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\pi}
=
\boldsymbol{\pi}\mathbf{P},
\qquad
\pi_S+\pi_F=1.
\label{eq:ris_stationary_equations}
\end{equation}

For the two-state chain, the stationary probability of reliable operation is

\begin{equation}
\pi_S
=
\frac{
p_{FS}
}{
p_{FS}+p_{SF}
}.
\label{eq:ris_stationary_success}
\end{equation}

The predicted QoS and recent packet-reception history are used to update
the transition probabilities and hence estimate the long-term reliability
of the currently reserved BR.

The resource-retention decision can then be represented as

\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{BR}}(t)
=
\begin{cases}
\mathrm{retain},
&
\pi_S
\geq
\Gamma_{\mathrm{DTMC}},
\\
\mathrm{reselect},
&
\pi_S
<
\Gamma_{\mathrm{DTMC}},
\end{cases}
\label{eq:ris_dtmc_decision}
\end{equation}

where $\Gamma_{\mathrm{DTMC}}$ denotes the minimum acceptable
long-term reliability.

This mechanism prevents poor resources from being repeatedly retained
solely because of the conventional keep probability.

% ------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Wireless Blind-Spot Probability}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

A further performance indicator considered in the framework is the
Wireless Blind-Spot Probability (WBSP). A wireless blind spot occurs
when two mutually hidden vehicles cannot decode or sense each other's
resource reservations and consequently continue to select overlapping
resources.

Let $\mathcal{B}_{ij}$ denote the event that vehicles $i$ and $j$ are
mutually hidden and let $\mathcal{C}_{ij}$ denote the event that they
select conflicting resources. The WBSP can be represented as

\begin{equation}
P_{\mathrm{WBSP}}
=
\Pr
\left(
\mathcal{B}_{ij}
\cap
\mathcal{C}_{ij}
\right).
\label{eq:ris_wbsp}
\end{equation}

The RIS-assisted propagation path can reduce
$P_{\mathrm{WBSP}}$ by improving link visibility between vehicles that
would otherwise experience severe blockage.

\subsubsection{Predictive RIS-Assisted MAC Procedure}
% ------------------------------------------------------------

The overall procedure combines sidelink sensing, QoS prediction,
RIS activation, and DTMC-based resource-retention control.

\begin{algorithm}[!t]
\caption{Predictive RIS-Assisted Resource Retention for NR-V2X}
\label{alg:ris_predictive_mac}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]

\Require Current sidelink observations, reserved BR $B_i(t)$,
$P_{\mathrm{keep}}$, QoS thresholds, and DTMC parameters

\State Perform sidelink sensing and construct the candidate-resource set

\State Collect the current feature vector $\mathbf{x}(t)$

\State Predict
$\widehat{\mathrm{PRR}}(t+1)$ and
$\widehat{\mathrm{CBR}}(t+1)$ using the trained ML model

\If{
$\widehat{\mathrm{PRR}}(t+1)<\Gamma_{\mathrm{PRR}}$
\textbf{or}
$\widehat{\mathrm{CBR}}(t+1)>\Gamma_{\mathrm{CBR}}$
\textbf{or}
$I_{\mathrm{SI}}(t)>\Gamma_{\mathrm{SI}}$
}
    \State Activate RIS assistance
\Else
    \State Continue using the direct V2V link
\EndIf

\If{
$\widehat{\mathrm{PRR}}(t+1)
\geq
\Gamma_{\mathrm{PRR}}$
}
    \State Retain $B_i(t)$ with probability $P_{\mathrm{keep}}$
\Else
    \State Update the two-state DTMC transition probabilities
    \State Compute stationary reliability $\pi_S$
    \If{$\pi_S < \Gamma_{\mathrm{DTMC}}$}
        \State Trigger immediate BR reselection
    \Else
        \State Retain the current BR
    \EndIf
\EndIf

\State Update sidelink observations and repeat

\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

% ============================================================
\section{Summary}
\label{sec:vehicular_summary}
% ============================================================

Vehicular networking has progressed from distributed VANET architectures
toward increasingly sophisticated V2X systems. IEEE 802.11p and DSRC
established the foundation for direct short-range vehicular communication,
particularly for safety-message dissemination. However, contention,
congestion, and limited coordination motivated the investigation of
cellular-based alternatives.

LTE-V2X introduced direct PC5 sidelink communication together with both
network-controlled and autonomous resource allocation. NR-V2X subsequently
extended this framework to support advanced applications, flexible
numerologies, unicast and groupcast communication, and more sophisticated
resource-allocation mechanisms.

The continuing evolution toward the Internet of Vehicles is integrating V2X
communication with sensing, computing, artificial intelligence, and automated
driving. Reliable safety-message dissemination and efficient radio resource
allocation therefore remain central problems in the design of future
vehicular communication networks.
