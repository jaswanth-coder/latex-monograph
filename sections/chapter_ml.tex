\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:ml_intro}
% ============================================================

The preceding chapters designed and analysed medium access protocols with mathematical models: Markov chains, queueing systems and optimisation problems whose parameters are assumed known. Such models give insight and provable guarantees, but they become hard to apply as networks grow more heterogeneous. A modern network carries traffic classes with conflicting requirements, its topology changes as nodes move or fail, and its environment includes interference and even deliberate attacks that no closed-form model captures completely. In these settings the designer often cannot write down the system dynamics accurately, yet the network itself generates large amounts of data about how it behaves. \emph{Machine learning} (ML) provides a way to turn this data into decisions.

This chapter presents six case studies, drawn from work carried out at IIT Madras, in which learning-based methods solve concrete problems in wireless and wired networks. The problems fall into two groups. The first group concerns \emph{resource allocation and scheduling}: deciding which node transmits in which time--frequency cell, and how radio resources are shared between services with different demands. The second group concerns \emph{classification}: identifying the modulation format of a received signal, and deciding whether network traffic is benign or malicious. Before turning to the case studies, the remainder of this section introduces the learning paradigms they use.

\subsection{Supervised Learning for Classification}
In supervised learning, a model is trained on a labelled dataset $\mathcal{D}=\{(\mathbf{x}_n,y_n)\}_{n=1}^{N_D}$, where $\mathbf{x}_n$ is a feature vector (for instance, statistics of a traffic flow, or samples of a received waveform) and $y_n\in\{1,\dots,C\}$ is its class label. A neural network with parameters $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ maps the input to a vector of scores $\mathbf{z}=f_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x})$, which the \emph{softmax} function converts into class probabilities,
\begin{equation}
  p_c(\mathbf{x}) = \frac{e^{z_c}}{\sum_{j=1}^{C} e^{z_j}}, \qquad c=1,\dots,C.
  \label{eq:ml_softmax}
\end{equation}
The parameters are chosen to minimise the \emph{cross-entropy} loss
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}) = -\frac{1}{N_D}\sum_{n=1}^{N_D}\log p_{y_n}(\mathbf{x}_n),
  \label{eq:ml_crossentropy}
\end{equation}
usually by stochastic gradient descent or one of its adaptive variants such as Adam. For binary problems ($C=2$) the softmax reduces to the logistic sigmoid $\phi(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), which apply small learnable filters across their input, are particularly effective when the input has spatial or temporal structure, as in a sampled radio waveform.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning for Sequential Decisions}
Scheduling decisions differ from classification in one important respect: each decision changes the state of the network, and therefore affects the decisions that follow. Such problems are modelled as a \emph{Markov decision process} (MDP), defined by the tuple $(\mathcal{S},\mathcal{A},\mathcal{P},R,\gamma)$. At each step $t$ an agent observes the state $s_t\in\mathcal{S}$ (for example, queue lengths and channel conditions), chooses an action $a_t\in\mathcal{A}$ (for example, a cell allocation), receives a reward $r_t=R(s_t,a_t)$, and the system moves to a new state according to the transition law $\mathcal{P}(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$. The agent seeks a policy $\pi(a\mid s)$ that maximises the expected discounted return
\begin{equation}
  J(\pi)=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\!\left[\sum_{t=0}^{\infty}\gamma^{t}r_t\right], \qquad 0\le\gamma<1.
  \label{eq:ml_return}
\end{equation}
The quality of an action is summarised by the action-value function $Q^{\pi}(s,a)$, the expected return obtained by taking action $a$ in state $s$ and following $\pi$ thereafter. The optimal action-value function satisfies the Bellman optimality equation~\cite{ml_sutton2018}
\begin{equation}
  Q^{*}(s,a)=\mathbb{E}\!\left[r_t+\gamma\max_{a'}Q^{*}(s_{t+1},a')\,\middle|\,s_t=s,\,a_t=a\right].
  \label{eq:ml_bellman}
\end{equation}
Reinforcement learning (RL) solves the MDP from interaction alone, without knowledge of $\mathcal{P}$. Two families of methods appear in this chapter. \emph{Value-based} methods, such as the deep Q-network (DQN)~\cite{ml_mnih2015}, approximate $Q^{*}$ with a neural network and act greedily with respect to it. \emph{Policy-gradient} methods instead parameterise the policy $\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ directly and adjust $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ along an estimate of $\nabla_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}J$. In the \emph{actor--critic} form, a second network (the critic) learns the state-value function $V(s)$, which is used to judge whether an action turned out better or worse than expected.

\subsection{Population-Based Search}
Some network design problems, such as building a conflict-free transmission schedule, are combinatorial and NP-hard. For these, nature-inspired \emph{population-based} methods are an attractive alternative to gradient-based learning. A population of candidate solutions is maintained, and better candidates gradually pass their features on to weaker ones, so that the population as a whole improves. Biogeography-based optimisation~\cite{ml_simon2008bbo}, used in Section~\ref{sec:ml_bios}, belongs to this family.

\section{Biogeography-Inspired Scheduling for TSCH Networks}
\label{sec:ml_bios}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Background}
The Industrial Internet of Things (IIoT) connects large numbers of sensors, controllers and actuators on a factory floor. Most of these devices run on batteries and cannot keep their radios on for long. To meet the reliability and energy requirements of such networks, the IEEE 802.15.4e standard defines a medium access mode called \emph{Time Slotted Channel Hopping} (TSCH). In TSCH, time is divided into slots indexed by $t=0,1,2,\dots$ and the bandwidth into $M$ orthogonal channels indexed by $k$. The pair $(t,k)$ is called a \emph{cell}, and it is the basic unit of communication. A group of $T$ consecutive slots forms a \emph{slotframe}, which repeats periodically. A node switches its radio on only in the cells in which it is scheduled to transmit or receive, and sleeps otherwise.

The standard specifies how a schedule is executed but not how it is computed. Designing a good schedule is therefore left to the implementer, and it is a hard problem: building a conflict-free schedule for data collection is NP-hard. Existing schedulers either place cells heuristically, which leads to collisions, or do not guarantee freedom from conflicts. The default Minimal Scheduling Function (MSF), for instance, chooses cell positions pseudo-randomly. Bommisetty and Venkatesh~\cite{ml_bios2021} approached the problem from a different direction, by treating schedule design as a search over a population of candidate schedules.

\subsection{Network Model and Constraints}
Consider $N$ nodes that send data over multiple hops to a single border router. The routing protocol for low-power and lossy networks (RPL) organises the nodes into a tree, called a destination-oriented directed acyclic graph (DODAG). Each node $i$ forwards its packets to its parent $p(i)$, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_bios_topology_schedule}. A schedule is a $T\times M$ matrix whose entries are the links $(i,p(i))$ active in each cell. The border router knows the topology and the queue length $q_i$ of every node, and computes the schedule centrally. Let $c_i$ denote the number of cells allocated to node $i$ in a slotframe.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{ML_chapter_figures/bios_topology_schedule.png}
\caption{An example RPL DODAG with border router $n_0$ (left) and a sample slotframe schedule for it (right).}
\label{fig:ml_bios_topology_schedule}
\end{figure}

The constraints on a schedule are divided into two kinds. \emph{Primary constraints} define what is physically feasible and may never be violated:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{PC1:} a node cannot be scheduled on two channels in the same slot, since its radio tunes to one channel at a time;
  \item \textbf{PC2:} neighbouring nodes cannot both transmit in the same slot, since a half-duplex node cannot transmit and receive simultaneously;
  \item \textbf{PC3:} two children of the same parent cannot share a slot, since the parent can receive only one packet at a time.
\end{itemize}
\emph{Secondary constraints} describe a good schedule rather than a feasible one. A schedule that violates them is still valid, but it incurs a penalty. They are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Interference (SC1):} nodes within interference range should not use the same cell. The penalty counts the transmissions whose SINR at the receiver falls below the decoding threshold $\beta$:
  \begin{equation}
    F_{SC1}=\sum_{i}\mathbb{1}\!\left(\mathrm{SINR}\bigl(i,\mathcal{N}(i)\bigr)\le\beta\right),
  \end{equation}
  where $\mathcal{N}(i)$ is the set of nodes scheduled in the same cell as node $i$.
  \item \textbf{Fairness (SC2):} the number of cells given to a node should be proportional to its backlog. The penalty follows the proportional-fairness metric:
  \begin{equation}
    F_{SC2}=\sum_{i}\log\!\left(\frac{q_i}{c_i}\right).
  \end{equation}
  \item \textbf{Buffering delay (SC3):} a packet waits at each relay until the next hop's cell arrives, so the slots allotted to consecutive hops should be close together. The penalty $F_{SC3}$ is the average gap $|t_1-t_2|$ between the slots of neighbouring nodes.
\end{itemize}
The quality of a schedule $S$ is measured by its total penalty, or \emph{regret},
\begin{equation}
  F(S)=F_{SC1}+F_{SC2}+F_{SC3},
  \label{eq:ml_bios_regret}
\end{equation}
and the design problem is to find a schedule that satisfies PC1--PC3 and minimises $F(S)$.

\subsection{The BIOS Algorithm}
Biogeography studies how species distribute themselves among habitats. A habitat with a high suitability (food, climate, vegetation) has many species. Species emigrate from it to poorer habitats, which in turn have a high immigration rate. Biogeography-based optimisation~\cite{ml_simon2008bbo} exploits this analogy. Each candidate solution is a habitat, its features are the ``suitability index variables'', and good solutions share their features with poor ones through migration. In the BIOS (Biogeography-Inspired Optimal Scheduling) algorithm, each candidate is a complete TSCH schedule, each node's cell assignment is one feature, and the regret $F(S)$ of \eqref{eq:ml_bios_regret} serves as the \emph{schedule regret index} (SRI). A schedule with a low SRI is a good habitat.

The algorithm first generates $R_{\max}$ random feasible schedules and ranks them by SRI. Candidate $S_i$, with rank $R_i$ (the best schedule having the highest rank), is assigned an immigration rate $\lambda_i$ and an emigration rate $\mu_i$:
\begin{equation}
  \lambda_i=I\left(1-\frac{R_i}{R_{\max}}\right), \qquad
  \mu_i=E\,\frac{R_i}{R_{\max}},
  \label{eq:ml_bios_migration}
\end{equation}
where $I$ and $E$ are the maximum immigration and emigration rates. A good schedule thus readily shares its features but seldom adopts others, while a poor schedule readily adopts features from others. Each iteration then performs three operations.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Migration.} For every node $k$ in schedule $S_i$, with probability $\lambda_i$ a donor schedule $S_j$ is picked, and with probability $\mu_j$ node $k$ is moved to the cells it occupies in $S_j$, provided the primary constraints remain satisfied.
  \item \textbf{Mutation.} With a small probability, either one node's cell is moved to another feasible cell, or the cells of two non-neighbouring nodes are swapped. Mutation keeps the population diverse. Without it, all candidates would collapse onto the current best schedule and could not adapt when the topology changes.
  \item \textbf{Differential update.} Instead of copying the best schedule $S_1$ wholesale, each candidate moves part of the way towards it,
  \begin{equation}
    S_i \leftarrow (1-\alpha)\,S_i+\alpha\,(S_1-S_i),
  \end{equation}
  where the learning rate $\alpha$ balances improvement against diversity: $\alpha=1$ would replace $S_i$ by $S_1$, and $\alpha=0$ would leave it unchanged.
\end{enumerate}
Because every candidate is generated and modified within the region defined by PC1--PC3, the schedule returned by BIOS is conflict-free by construction.

\subsection{Performance}
BIOS was implemented in the 6TiSCH simulator for a 50-node network with at most three hops, a 101-slot slotframe, 50 candidate schedules and Poisson traffic, and compared with MSF. Table~\ref{tab:ml_bios_results} lists the results. Since BIOS schedules contain no MAC-layer conflicts, every packet is delivered, whereas the pseudo-random placement of MSF loses packets to collisions. BIOS also attains a lower end-to-end delay and a lower radio duty cycle in every scenario. Overall, delay improves by about 60\% and energy efficiency by up to 40\%.

\section{Phasic Policy Gradient for IIoT Resource Allocation}
\label{sec:ml_ppg}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Background}
BIOS computes a schedule by searching a population of candidates. A complementary approach is to \emph{learn} a scheduling policy that maps the current network state directly to an allocation. Deep reinforcement learning is a natural tool for this, but the two obvious architectures both have drawbacks. Fully distributed agents see only local information, while a fully centralised agent faces a very large state space. Actor--critic methods offer a middle ground, but the usual practice of sharing one neural network between the actor (policy) and the critic (value function) lets the two objectives interfere with each other. The \emph{phasic policy gradient} (PPG) method of Cobbe \emph{et al.}~\cite{ml_cobbe2021ppg} removes this interference by training the policy and the value function in separate phases. Bommisetty and Venkatesh~\cite{ml_ppg2022} applied PPG to joint cell and power allocation in TSCH networks.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
The network model is that of Section~\ref{sec:ml_bios}. Let $X^{i}_{(t,k)}\in\{0,1\}$ indicate whether node $i$ is scheduled in cell $(t,k)$, and let $p^{i}$ be its transmit power. A packet of node $i$ fails if it waits longer than its deadline $D_i$, which happens with probability $P^{i}_{k,drop}=\Pr(W_i>D_i)$, or if co-channel interference pushes its SINR $\Gamma^{i}_{k}$ below the threshold $\beta$, which happens with probability $P^{i}_{k,err}=\Pr(\Gamma^{i}_{k}<\beta)$. The probability that the packet succeeds is therefore
\begin{equation}
  P^{i}_{k,suc}=1-P^{i}_{k,drop}-P^{i}_{k,err}+P^{i}_{k,drop}P^{i}_{k,err}.
\end{equation}
Network throughput and energy efficiency (successful deliveries per unit transmit power) follow as
\begin{equation}
  Th=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{M}P^{i}_{k,suc}, \qquad
  \eta=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{M}P^{i}_{k,suc}}{\sum_{i=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{M}p^{i}X^{i}_{(t,k)}}.
\end{equation}
The schedule $\mathbf{X}$ and the power vector $\mathbf{P}$ are chosen to maximise a weighted utility subject to the radio and quality-of-service constraints:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \max_{\mathbf{P},\mathbf{X}}\quad & U=\theta_1\,Th+\theta_2\,\eta \\
  \text{s.t.}\quad & \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^{M}X^{i}_{(t,k)}\le 1, && \forall i, \\
  & P^{i}_{k,drop}<\epsilon_i,\quad P^{i}_{k,err}<\delta_i, && \forall i,k.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:ml_ppg_problem}
\end{equation}
The first constraint states that a node uses at most one channel per slot. The other two bound the deadline-violation and error probabilities. The weights $\theta_1,\theta_2$ set the relative importance of throughput and energy efficiency.

To solve \eqref{eq:ml_ppg_problem} with RL, the state is $s=\{\theta_1,\theta_2,\mathbf{q},\mathcal{T}\}$, consisting of the utility weights, the vector of queue lengths and the current topology. The action is the cell and power choice $\{(X^{i}_{(t,k)},p^{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}$ of every node, and the reward at each step is the utility $U$.

\subsection{Learning in Two Phases}
PPG alternates between two phases.

\textbf{Policy phase.} The policy network $\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}$ is trained with proximal policy optimisation (PPO)~\cite{ml_schulman2017ppo}. Let $x_t(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(a_t\mid s_t)/\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{old}}(a_t\mid s_t)$ be the probability ratio between the new and old policies, and $\hat{A}_t$ an estimate of the advantage of action $a_t$, that is, how much better it was than the average action in $s_t$. The policy maximises the clipped objective
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
  L^{policy}(\boldsymbol{\theta})={}&\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\min\bigl(x_t(\boldsymbol{\theta})\hat{A}_t,\;\mathrm{clip}\bigl(x_t(\boldsymbol{\theta}),1-\zeta,1+\zeta\bigr)\hat{A}_t\bigr)\Bigr]\\
  &-\nu\,D_{KL}\bigl(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{old}}\,\|\,\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\bigr).
\end{split}
\end{equation}
Clipping the ratio to $[1-\zeta,1+\zeta]$, together with the Kullback--Leibler penalty, keeps each update close to the previous policy and so stabilises learning. In the same phase, a separate value network $V_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}$ is fitted to the observed returns $\hat{V}$:
\begin{equation}
  L^{value}(\boldsymbol{\phi})=\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl(V_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(s_t)-\hat{V}(s_t)\bigr)^{2}\Bigr].
\end{equation}

\textbf{Auxiliary phase.} The value network learns useful features of the network state, and the auxiliary phase \emph{distils} these features into the policy network without disturbing the policy already learnt. The policy network is trained on the joint objective
\begin{equation}
  L^{joint}=L^{aux}+\mu_{clone}\,D_{KL}\bigl(\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}_{old}}\,\|\,\pi_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}\bigr),
\end{equation}
where $L^{aux}$ is an auxiliary loss (here the value loss, as in~\cite{ml_cobbe2021ppg}) and the behavioural-cloning term, weighted by $\mu_{clone}$, prevents the policy from drifting. Separating the two phases lets each network be trained with its own number of updates and its own sample reuse, which is the main advantage of PPG over a shared actor--critic.

\subsection{Performance}
The scheme was evaluated in the 6TiSCH simulator for networks of up to 70 nodes and at most three hops, against a PPO-based scheduler and the default MSF. Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_ppg_results} (left) shows that PPG earns a higher reward than PPO from the start of training and converges faster. Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_ppg_results} (right) shows the system throughput as the network grows. More nodes mean more interference on every link, so throughput falls for all schemes, but PPG remains well ahead, improving throughput by 58\% over MSF and by 22\% over PPO.

\section{Deep Q-Learning for eMBB and URLLC Coexistence in 5G}
\label{sec:ml_dqn}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Background}
5G New Radio serves three classes of traffic: enhanced mobile broadband (eMBB), which needs high data rates; ultra-reliable low-latency communication (URLLC), which needs delivery within about a millisecond with very high reliability; and massive machine-type communication. In industrial deployments, many latency-critical URLLC devices must share the spectrum with eMBB users that are continuously backlogged. The standard way to accommodate a URLLC packet is \emph{puncturing}: the resources already granted to an eMBB user are overwritten by a short URLLC transmission lasting a mini-slot, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_dqn_frame}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.62\textwidth]{ML_chapter_figures/dqn_puncturing_frame.png}
\caption{A 5G subframe (numerology 1) in which URLLC mini-slot transmissions puncture the resource blocks of eMBB users.}
\label{fig:ml_dqn_frame}
\end{figure}

Puncturing is fast, but it is not free. Every punctured resource block corrupts part of an eMBB codeword, so the eMBB user's decoding error probability rises and more retransmissions are needed. When URLLC arrivals are numerous, as in massive URLLC, eMBB reliability collapses. The opposite extreme, reserving dedicated (orthogonal) resources for URLLC, wastes spectrum when URLLC traffic is light. Desai, Indulekha and Venkatesh~\cite{ml_dqn2025} proposed a hybrid scheme in which a DQN agent decides, slot by slot, how much spectrum to reserve for URLLC and how much to leave to puncturing.

\subsection{System Model}
Let $N$ be the number of physical resource blocks (PRBs) in a mini-slot. In the hybrid scheme, $K$ of them are reserved orthogonally for URLLC, and the remaining $N-K$ carry eMBB data that URLLC may puncture. URLLC requests arrive as a Poisson process of rate $\lambda_u$, and eMBB users are fully buffered. Let $\varphi(t)=B\log_2(1+\mathrm{SNR})$ denote the capacity of one PRB. If $l$ mini-slots are punctured, an eMBB user's instantaneous rate is
\begin{equation}
  R_{X_e}(t)=\sum_{k=K+1}^{N}\mathbb{1}_p(k)\,\varphi(t)\left[1-\frac{l}{N-k}\right],
  \label{eq:ml_dqn_embb}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbb{1}_p(k)$ marks the shared PRBs allocated to that user. A URLLC user collects the punctured share of the shared PRBs together with any reserved PRBs $\mathbb{1}_s(k)$ assigned to it:
\begin{equation}
  R_{X_u}(t)=\sum_{k=K+1}^{N}\mathbb{1}_p(k)\,\varphi(t)\,\frac{l}{N-k}+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\mathbb{1}_s(k)\,\varphi(t).
  \label{eq:ml_dqn_urllc}
\end{equation}
Summing over users and slots gives the cumulative eMBB and URLLC throughputs $R_e$ and $R_u$. Writing $\alpha\in[0,1]$ for the fraction of shared PRBs punctured, the design problem is to serve as much URLLC traffic as possible while protecting eMBB:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \max_{\alpha\in[0,1]}\quad & R_{X_u}(t)=(N-K)\,\alpha\,\varphi(t)+K\,\varphi(t) \\
  \text{s.t.}\quad & R_{X_e}(t)=(N-K)(1-\alpha)\,\varphi(t)\ge R^{eMBB}_{\min}, \\
  & P^{eMBB}_{out}(\alpha)\le\epsilon_{eMBB},\qquad P^{URLLC}_{out}\le\epsilon_{URLLC},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $P_{out}$ denotes the probability that a service misses its rate, latency or reliability target.

\subsection{The DQN Scheduler}
Because URLLC arrivals are random and the right value of $K$ depends on the current load, the problem is cast as an MDP and solved with a deep Q-network (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_dqn_model}). The state in slot $t$ is
\begin{equation}
  s_t=\bigl[R_{X_e}(t),\,R_{X_u}(t),\,\lambda_u,\,\lambda_e,\,L_t(\lambda_u)\bigr],
\end{equation}
where $L_t(\lambda_u)$ is the number of URLLC requests waiting in the queue. The action is the number of reserved PRBs, $K\in\{0,1,\dots,N\}$. The reward
\begin{equation}
  r_t=c_1R_e+c_2L_t(\lambda_u)+c_3
  \label{eq:ml_dqn_reward}
\end{equation}
balances eMBB throughput against URLLC backlog, which is a direct proxy for URLLC latency. The weights $c_1,c_2$ normalise the two terms, and the constant $c_3$ shifts the reward so that good decisions are rewarded positively and poor ones negatively.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{ML_chapter_figures/dqn_model.png}
\caption{Structure of the DQN-based scheduler. The agent observes the state $S_t$ and chooses the number $K$ of PRBs reserved for URLLC.}
\label{fig:ml_dqn_model}
\end{figure}

The Q-network $Q(s,a;\boldsymbol{\theta})$ outputs one value for each candidate $K$. Training follows the standard DQN recipe~\cite{ml_mnih2015}. Transitions $(s,a,r,s')$ are stored in a replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$ and sampled in random mini-batches, which breaks the correlation between consecutive slots. Targets are computed with a separate, periodically synchronised target network $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{-}$,
\begin{equation}
  y=r+\gamma\max_{a'}Q(s',a';\boldsymbol{\theta}^{-}),
\end{equation}
and the network is trained to minimise the temporal-difference error
\begin{equation}
  L(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a,r,s')\sim\mathcal{D}}\Bigl[\bigl(y-Q(s,a;\boldsymbol{\theta})\bigr)^{2}\Bigr].
\end{equation}
Exploration uses an $\epsilon$-greedy rule. Algorithm~\ref{alg:ml_dqn} summarises the procedure. A deliberately small network (two layers) keeps the scheduler light enough for per-slot decisions.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\caption{DQN-based hybrid PRB allocation for eMBB and URLLC}
\label{alg:ml_dqn}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State Initialise replay buffer $\mathcal{D}$, Q-network $\boldsymbol{\theta}$ and target network $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{-}\gets\boldsymbol{\theta}$
\For{each episode}
  \State Observe initial state $s_t$
  \For{each mini-slot}
    \State With probability $\epsilon$ pick a random $K\in\{0,\dots,N\}$; otherwise $K\gets\arg\max_a Q(s_t,a;\boldsymbol{\theta})$
    \State Reserve $K$ PRBs for URLLC; share the remaining $N-K$ with eMBB (puncturing if needed)
    \State Compute $R_{X_e}(t)$, $R_{X_u}(t)$ from \eqref{eq:ml_dqn_embb}--\eqref{eq:ml_dqn_urllc} and $r_t$ from \eqref{eq:ml_dqn_reward}
    \State Observe $s_{t+1}$; store $(s_t,K,r_t,s_{t+1})$ in $\mathcal{D}$
    \State Sample a mini-batch from $\mathcal{D}$, form targets $y_j$, and take a gradient step on $L(\boldsymbol{\theta})$
    \State $s_t\gets s_{t+1}$
  \EndFor
  \State Every $T_{\mathrm{sync}}$ episodes, set $\boldsymbol{\theta}^{-}\gets\boldsymbol{\theta}$
\EndFor
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Performance}
The scheduler was trained on a 10\,MHz carrier with numerology~1, 52 PRBs per symbol, 10 fully buffered eMBB users and URLLC arrival rates $\lambda_u$ from 5 to 100 requests per symbol. It was compared with a pure puncturing baseline. Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_dqn_latency} shows the average URLLC latency. Under puncturing, the delay grows sharply with load and exceeds 8\,ms at high arrival rates. The DQN scheduler keeps latency much lower over the entire range, because it reserves more PRBs as soon as the queue starts to build up.

\centering

\caption{Average URLLC latency of the DQN scheduler and the puncturing baseline, as a function of the URLLC arrival rate.}
\label{fig:ml_dqn_latency}
\end{figure}

The price of this protection is paid by eMBB traffic, as Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_dqn_tradeoff} shows. As $\lambda_u$ increases, the agent shifts resources towards URLLC: URLLC throughput rises until it saturates, while eMBB throughput declines gradually rather than collapsing. Even the DQN scheduler eventually saturates at very high loads, where the total bandwidth simply cannot serve all requests.

\section{Hybrid Deep Networks for Automatic Modulation Classification}
\label{sec:ml_amc}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Background}
\emph{Automatic modulation classification} (AMC) is the task of identifying the modulation scheme of a received signal without prior knowledge of the transmitter's settings. It is a key enabler of cognitive radio, dynamic spectrum access and interference management. Classical AMC is either likelihood-based, which is optimal but computationally heavy and sensitive to model mismatch, or feature-based, relying on hand-crafted statistics such as cumulants. Deep learning replaces hand-crafted features by features learnt directly from the raw in-phase and quadrature (I/Q) samples. Two CNN families have been particularly successful. VGG-style networks~\cite{ml_oshea2018} use stacks of small convolutions. RanNet~\cite{ml_rannet2022} adds \emph{residual} and \emph{attention} structures. Each has weaknesses across the full range of signal-to-noise ratios (SNRs). Prajapati \emph{et al.}~\cite{ml_amc2024} designed hybrid architectures that combine their strengths.

\subsection{Building Blocks}
Three ideas underlie the hybrid models.

\textbf{Skip (residual) connections.} Very deep networks are hard to train because gradients shrink as they propagate back through many layers. A residual block learns a correction $\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})$ to its input and outputs
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{y}=\mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x})+\mathbf{x},
\end{equation}
so that information and gradients can bypass the block whenever the correction is not useful.

\textbf{Channel attention.} RanNet's RanBlock first extracts fine features $\mathbf{F}$ with parallel $1\times1$, $1\times3$ and $1\times5$ convolutions (the FeaBlock). An attention module then computes one weight per feature channel by global average pooling, $1\times1$ convolutions and a sigmoid, and rescales the features with these weights. Informative channels are thereby strengthened and irrelevant ones suppressed:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{a}=\sigma\bigl(\mathrm{Conv}_{1\times1}(\mathrm{GAP}(\mathbf{F}))\bigr),\qquad
  \mathbf{y}=\mathbf{a}\odot\mathbf{F}+\mathbf{x}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{The X block.} Inspired by the chained residual pooling of RefineNet~\cite{ml_refinenet2017}, the X block (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_amc_xblock}) is a chain of $3\times3$ convolutions in which each convolution acts on the output of the previous one and every output is added back to the main path:
\begin{equation}
  \mathbf{c}_0=\mathrm{ReLU}(\mathbf{x}),\qquad
  \mathbf{c}_j=\mathrm{Conv}_j(\mathbf{c}_{j-1}),\qquad
  \mathbf{y}=\mathbf{c}_0+\sum_{j=1}^{4}\mathbf{c}_j.
\end{equation}
Each successive convolution sees a wider region of the input, so the block gathers context from large regions without a large pooling window.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{ML_chapter_figures/amc_xblock.png}
\caption{The X block: a chain of $3\times3$ convolutions whose outputs are summed with residual connections.}
\label{fig:ml_amc_xblock}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Hybrid Architectures}
Three hybrids were constructed, each starting from the RanNet pre-processing block (two strided convolutions with batch normalisation):
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Hybrid~1} inserts a VGG-style convolution and max-pooling layer, with a skip connection, between two RanBlocks, and ends with a further convolution and pooling stage. The extra VGG layers retain information during early processing (145,322 parameters).
  \item \textbf{Hybrid~2} keeps one RanBlock and appends an X block, combining channel attention with chained residual convolutions (209,578 parameters).
  \item \textbf{Hybrid~3} replaces the FeaBlock inside both RanBlocks by X blocks, which gives the richest but also the largest model (332,074 parameters).
\end{itemize}
All models were trained on the RadioML 2016.10b dataset, which contains ten modulation schemes (including BPSK, QPSK, 8PSK, 16-QAM, 64-QAM, PAM4, GFSK and WBFM), using SNRs from $-10$ to $+18$\,dB, a 70:30 train--test split, the Adam optimiser and the cross-entropy loss~\eqref{eq:ml_crossentropy}. Accuracy is the fraction of test frames whose modulation is correctly identified.

\subsection{Performance}
Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_amc_hybrid_comparison} compares the three hybrids across SNR, and Table~\ref{tab:ml_amc_results} lists accuracy at 18\,dB together with model size. Hybrid~1 and Hybrid~2 track RanNet closely and clearly outperform VGG, particularly at low SNR, while using far fewer parameters than VGG. Hybrid~1 is slightly better than Hybrid~2 at very low SNR. Hybrid~3, despite having the most parameters, is the least accurate. Additional depth beyond a point leads to overfitting rather than better features, so architectural balance matters more than size. The confusion matrices of the paper show that the remaining errors are concentrated in 16-QAM versus 64-QAM, two dense constellations that look alike in noise.

\section{Intrusion Detection with Efficient Feature Selection}
\label{sec:ml_features}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Background}
A network intrusion detection system (NIDS) monitors traffic and raises an alarm when it detects malicious activity. Conventional NIDS use signatures of known attacks or hand-built statistical rules, and every attack class must be handled separately. Machine learning recasts intrusion detection as a classification problem: given the features of a traffic flow, decide whether it is benign or an attack. The model is learnt from data rather than written by hand.

The quality of such a classifier depends heavily on its features. Widely used datasets such as KDD Cup 99, NSL-KDD and UNSW-NB15 consist mostly of \emph{primary} features, which are read directly from packets: protocol, service, bytes transferred, flags and so on. \emph{Derived} features are obtained by applying statistical transformations to primary features over time. Examples are the standard deviation of packet sizes, the Fourier transform of inbound packet sizes, and the number of inbound packets in each of several time intervals. They describe the \emph{behaviour} of a flow and are far more revealing. The Advanced Security Network Metrics (ASNM) datasets provide close to 900 such features. The difficulty is that so many features make the classifier slow to train and prone to overfitting. Desai and Venkatesh~\cite{ml_features2023} showed that a simple statistical filter can remove most of them without loss of accuracy.

\subsection{Chi-Square Feature Selection}
After pre-processing (removing spoofable identifiers such as IP and MAC addresses, encoding categorical fields numerically, discarding constant features, and min--max normalising every feature to $[0,1]$), each feature is tested for statistical dependence on the class label with Pearson's chi-square test. For a given feature,
\begin{equation}
  \chi^{2}=\sum_{i}\frac{(O_i-E_i)^{2}}{E_i},
  \label{eq:ml_chi2}
\end{equation}
where $O_i$ is the observed number of attack samples taking the $i$-th value of the feature, and $E_i$ is the number expected if the feature and the label were independent. A large $\chi^{2}$ (equivalently, a small $p$-value) indicates that the feature carries information about the label. Features are ranked by $p$-value, and only the most dependent ones are kept, about 400 out of roughly 900. The top-ranked features turn out to be derived rather than primary features, mostly counts of inbound packets in particular time intervals and the count of inbound TCP SYN packets. This confirms that flow behaviour is what distinguishes attacks from normal traffic.

\subsection{Classifiers}
Two classifiers were built on the selected features. The first is logistic regression, which passes a weighted sum of the features through the sigmoid $\phi(z)=1/(1+e^{-z})$ to obtain the probability of an attack. The second is a multilayer perceptron with a single hidden layer and sigmoid activations, trained with the Adam optimiser at learning rate $10^{-5}$. The size of the hidden layer was chosen by grid search (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_ids_feature_selection}). For the ASNM-NBPO dataset, seven hidden nodes already reach the best accuracy. Performance is reported as precision $TP/(TP+FP)$, recall $TP/(TP+FN)$ and their harmonic mean, the F1 score, averaged over 5-fold stratified cross-validation.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.50\textwidth,height=5.0cm,keepaspectratio]{ML_chapter_figures/ids_feature_selection.png}
\caption{Classification accuracy against the number of hidden-layer nodes (ASNM-NBPO dataset).}
\label{fig:ml_ids_feature_selection}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Performance}
Table~\ref{tab:ml_ids_results} shows that, after feature selection, both classifiers achieve F1 scores above 99\% on all three ASNM datasets, well above the na\"ive Bayes classifier with forward feature selection proposed by the creators of ASNM. Feature selection costs almost nothing in accuracy, but it shrinks the input layer and therefore the computation per sample. The neural network trains 2.6$\times$ faster on ASNM-CDX-2009 and 5.6$\times$ faster on ASNM-NBPO. The lesson is that flow-based derived features give better detection than primary features, and that a simple filter such as the chi-square test makes them affordable.

\section{Robust Intrusion Detection for Previously Unseen Attacks}
\label{sec:ml_rnids}
% ============================================================

\subsection{Background}
A classifier can only recognise the classes it was trained on. When a genuinely new attack appears (in recent years, for example, the Mirai botnet and new UDP amplification attacks), a CNN-based NIDS must still assign it to one of its known classes, and in practice it labels most unseen attacks as \emph{benign}. This is the worst possible failure for a security system. Detecting such \emph{outliers} is usually treated as a separate problem from classifying known attacks. Approaches such as zero-shot learning require side information (attributes) that a new adversary does not provide. Desai and Venkatesh~\cite{ml_rnids2022} proposed a unified model, the Robust NIDS (RNIDS), that both classifies known attacks and flags unknown ones.

\subsection{The RNIDS Model}
RNIDS operates in two phases (Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_rnids_architecture}). In the \emph{training phase}, a compact CNN is trained on labelled traffic. In the \emph{deployment phase}, live traffic is classified by the CNN, and an outlier detector examines the CNN's output to decide whether the sample belongs to any known class at all. Samples flagged as new adversaries are stored and fed back for retraining, which closes the loop without human intervention.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1.00\textwidth,height=5.2cm,keepaspectratio]{ML_chapter_figures/rnids_architecture.png}
\caption{The RNIDS model: training phase and real-time deployment phase.}
\label{fig:ml_rnids_architecture}
\end{figure}

\textbf{The classifier.} The model is trained on the CICIDS2017 dataset~\cite{ml_sharafaldin2018}, which contains about 2.8 million labelled flows with 78 features each and covers attacks such as DDoS, several DoS variants, botnet, port scan, brute-force (FTP- and SSH-Patator) and web attacks. Each feature vector is min--max normalised, zero-padded to length 128 and reshaped into an $8\times16$ ``image''. Two convolutional layers ($3\times3$ kernels, 16 and 64 filters) with max pooling are followed by dense layers with SELU activations and a softmax output~\eqref{eq:ml_softmax}. The whole network has only 70,252 trainable parameters, which keeps inference fast. Classes were rebalanced by up- and down-sampling so that rare attacks are not ignored.

\textbf{The outlier detector.} For an input sample, the softmax output $\mathbf{p}=[p_1,\dots,p_C]$ is a point in a $C$-dimensional probability space. Samples of a known class form tight clusters in this space, because the CNN assigns them confident and consistent probabilities. A sample of an unseen attack produces an unusual probability vector that lies far from every cluster, as illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:ml_rnids_knn}. RNIDS stores the probability vectors of correctly classified training samples. For a new sample it finds the $K$ nearest stored points, with Euclidean distances $d_1,\dots,d_K$, and computes the average distance
\begin{equation}
  d_{avg}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}d_j.
\end{equation}
The sample is declared a \emph{new adversary} if $d_{avg}>d_{th}$, and is otherwise assigned the class predicted by the CNN. Tuning on a large sample set gave $K=12$ and $d_{th}=10^{-6}$.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.38\textwidth]{ML_chapter_figures/rnids_knn.png}
\caption{Outlier detection by $K$ nearest neighbours in the space of CNN output probabilities. Known attack classes form clusters, and an anomaly $a_1$ lies far from its neighbours.}
\label{fig:ml_rnids_knn}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Performance}
On known attacks, the CNN achieves an overall accuracy of 98.3\%. To test robustness, each attack class was removed in turn from the training set and presented only at test time. Table~\ref{tab:ml_rnids_results} compares the fraction of such unseen samples that the CNN alone still flags as some attack with the fraction that RNIDS flags as a new adversary. The CNN alone recognises an unseen attack only when it resembles a known one: a missing DoS variant is often caught as another DoS attack, but botnet, port-scan and SSH-Patator traffic are almost entirely passed as benign. With the outlier detector, these attacks are flagged in 75\%, 88\% and 90\% of cases, and most DoS variants in more than 99\% of cases. The main open issue is retraining. When new samples are fed back, the CNN must learn the new class without forgetting the old ones, and achieving this balance remains difficult.

\section{Summary}
\label{sec:ml_summary}
% ============================================================

The six case studies show machine learning entering network design at several layers. For scheduling and resource allocation, the decision problems are combinatorial and their environments change over time. Population-based search (BIOS) yields conflict-free TSCH schedules with lower delay and energy use. Deep RL learns allocation policies directly from experience, whether by phasic policy gradient for IIoT cell and power allocation or by a lightweight DQN that adapts the URLLC reservation in 5G to the instantaneous load. For classification, carefully designed hybrid CNNs identify modulation formats accurately with modest model size. In intrusion detection, statistically selected derived features give highly accurate and fast classifiers, and an outlier detector placed on top of a CNN lets the system recognise attacks it has never seen.

Several themes recur. Domain knowledge remains essential: the constraints of the TSCH schedule, the structure of the 5G frame and the choice of traffic features all shape what the learner can achieve. Model size must be kept small enough for real-time operation, and several of the designs above deliberately trade a little accuracy for speed. Finally, adaptivity, whether through mutation, continual policy updates or feedback of new attack samples, is what allows learning-based methods to cope with the changing conditions that defeat static protocols.
